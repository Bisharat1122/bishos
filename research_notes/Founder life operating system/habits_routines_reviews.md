# Habits, Routines, Energy, Focus and Review Rituals: Evidence for a Founder Life OS

*Evidence labels used below: **[STRONG]** = meta-analysis of randomized/experimental studies or very large field datasets; **[MODERATE]** = single well-designed experiment/field study, or meta-analysis with high risk of bias / correlational data; **[WEAK/POPULAR]** = practitioner framework, author anecdote, internal pilot, or contested theory. Research notes current to October 2026.*

## 1. What does research say about habit formation, streaks, tracking and why habits fail, and which design features help?

### Takeaway
Habits form through consistent repetition of a behavior in a stable context. It takes months, not 21 days, and the time varies widely by person and behavior: median about 59–66 days, with a range of 4–335. A single miss doesn't derail habit formation, but very inconsistent performance does. Streaks motivate people, but a broken streak sharply reduces persistence, so the strongest design features are forgiveness mechanics: planned skips or "emergency reserves", streak repair, and "never miss twice" recovery prompts. Pair these with if-then planning, which is the best-evidenced planning tool (d≈0.65), and with progress monitoring that is recorded and visible (d≈0.40).

### Cited Findings

**How long habit formation takes**
- [STRONG-ish, foundational] Lally et al. (Eur. J. Social Psychology, online 2009 / print 2010): 96 volunteers each picked one eating, drinking or activity behavior to do daily in the same context for 12 weeks, and 82 gave enough data for analysis. "Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process." Per Lally, "people who were very inconsistent in performing the behaviour did not succeed in making habits." Performing the behavior more consistently was associated with better model fit. — [UCL news, 2009](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [paper record (Crossref)](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- Lally: the median time to reach 95% of each person's automaticity plateau was 66 days, with individuals ranging from 18 to 254 days. Automaticity followed an asymptotic curve: big early gains, then a plateau. Simple behaviors such as drinking water became automatic faster than effortful ones such as 50 sit-ups. — [Spring.org.uk summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/2023/01/form-a-habit.php)
- [MODERATE] Singh, Murphy, Maher & Smith (2024), *Healthcare* 12:2488, a systematic review and meta-analysis of health-behavior habit formation. It covers 20 studies and 2,601 participants. Only 4 studies reported time to habit: medians of 59–66 days, means of 106–154 days, and an individual range of **4–335 days**. Most studies (11) had a high risk of bias. — [University of Adelaide record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006)
- [STRONG, big data] Buyalskaya, Ho, Milkman, Li, Duckworth & Camerer (PNAS, 2023) used machine learning on more than 12M gym check-ins and more than 40M hospital hand-washing events. They found "no magic number". Gym habits typically took **months** (press coverage: about 6 months on average), while hand-washing took **weeks**. For 76% of gym-goers, **time since the last visit** was an important predictor of attending again. Time of day had no effect on gym habit formation. More habitual (predictable) gym-goers were *less* responsive to an intervention meant to boost attendance. — [PNAS/PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10151500); [Caltech news](https://experts.caltech.edu/news/no-magic-number-for-time-it-takes-to-form-habits)

**Planning tools that raise follow-through**
- [STRONG] Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38:69–119, a meta-analysis of implementation intentions ("If situation X, then I will do Y"). It pooled 94 independent tests from 63 reports and found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, **d = .65**. The plans helped with starting goal striving, shielding goals from distraction, disengaging from failing courses of action, and conserving capacity. — [Univ. Konstanz record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f). One secondary blog says the effect was robust to publication-bias correction and covered more than 8,000 participants; that is unverified against the paper. — [goalsandprogress.com](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-research/)
- [STRONG] Wang, Wang & Gai (2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*), a meta-analysis of Mental Contrasting with Implementation Intentions (MCII, branded as WOOP). It covers 21 studies, 24 effect sizes and N = 15,907, with a small-to-medium effect on goal attainment (**g = 0.336**). Delivery through **interaction with an experimenter (g = 0.465)** beat delivery through **documents (g = 0.277)**. — [PMC8149892](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8149892/)
- Few studies have directly tested whether the full MCII combination beats mental contrasting or implementation intentions alone. — [HAL (Univ. Lille)](https://hal.univ-lille.fr/hal-04388496)
- WOOP steps, always in this order: **Wish** (what you want), **Outcome** (the best thing about getting it), **Obstacle** (what *in you* is most likely to get in the way), **Plan** ("If [obstacle], then I will [action]"). Oettingen's research shows that positive fantasy *alone* can hamper realization. The order matters: imagine the outcome first, then the obstacle. — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/intl/blog/between-cultures/202108/making-wishes-come-true); [KQED MindShift](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/38827/why-understanding-obstacles-is-essential-to-achieving-goals)
- Mental contrasting also helps people commit *less* to goals they don't expect to reach (Oettingen, Pak & Schnetter 2001). It works as a filter for goals as well as a booster. — [KQED MindShift](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/38827/why-understanding-obstacles-is-essential-to-achieving-goals)

**Streaks and tracking**
- [STRONG] Harkin et al. (2016, *Psychological Bulletin* 142:198–229), a meta-analysis of 138 RCTs (N = 19,951). Interventions that prompted monitoring of goal progress improved goal attainment, **d = 0.40** [0.32, 0.48], and increased monitoring frequency, d = 1.98. Effects were **larger when progress was reported/made public and when it was physically recorded**. Most studies concerned health goals. — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/); [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals.aspx)
- [MODERATE–STRONG] Silverman & Barasch (2023, *Journal of Consumer Research*), "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions". A broken streak makes people **less likely to continue the behavior, even when the break was caused by external events outside their control**. Under their "streaks as goals" account, making streaks salient through tracking or framing motivates preservation but **magnifies the negative effect of breaks**. The effect is amplified when people blame themselves for the break and **attenuated when the streak can be "repaired."** The authors suggest apps not focus on broken streaks and offer alternative ways to keep streaks going. — [UDSpace (paper)](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb); [UDaily](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- [MODERATE] Sharif & Shu, "Nudging persistence after failure through emergency reserves" (OBHDP, 1 field + 4 lab studies). Framing a goal with built-in slack helps people keep going after a missed subgoal. In a step-count field study the conditions were Easy (5 days/week), Hard (7 days/week), Reserve-Weekly (7 days with 2 skips per week) and Reserve-Monthly (7 days with 8 skips per month). Reserve participants reached their goals **up to 40% more** often than Easy or Hard participants. **Rebound rates after a missed day** were 55% (reserve-weekly) and 47% (reserve-monthly), vs **37% (hard)**. The mechanism is greater perceived progress, which sustains commitment. — [UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/)
- [MODERATE] Fresh-start effect (Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014, *Management Science*): Google searches for "diet", gym visits and goal commitments all rise after temporal landmarks. Gym card-reader data showed spikes at the **start of a week, start of a semester, and right after a birthday**. The proposed mechanism is that landmarks separate the "old self" from the "new self". — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/why-monday-is-the-best-day-for-setting-new-goals.html)

**Practitioner frameworks (popular; mechanisms partly evidence-backed)**
- [WEAK/POPULAR, consistent with research] James Clear, habit stacking: "After/Before [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]." The cue must be **highly specific and immediately actionable**. Clear's vague cue "When I take a break for lunch" failed; "When I close my laptop for lunch" worked. The cue should also **occur as often as the desired habit**: don't anchor a daily habit to a Monday-only cue. Avoid anchors at chaotic times. To find anchors, list the things you do daily without fail. — [jamesclear.com/habit-stacking](https://jamesclear.com/habit-stacking)
- Clear on habit tracking: a tracker gives "immediate evidence that you completed your habit," a visual cue, motivation ("The most effective form of motivation is progress") and immediate satisfaction. Caveats: tracking "can feel like a burden because it forces you into two habits"; "It is better to consistently track one habit than to sporadically track ten"; track immediately after the habit ("After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [TRACK MY HABIT]"). His recovery rule is "**Never miss twice.** Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the start of a new habit." He also gives the **Two-Minute Rule**: "scale your habits down until they take two minutes or less to perform." — [jamesclear.com/habit-tracker](https://jamesclear.com/habit-tracker)
- Critics argue Clear overstates the harm a second miss does. Lally's data only show that a *single* miss didn't matter, and "never miss twice" is a heuristic, not a tested threshold. — [critique, Substack](https://notionadam.substack.com/p/atomic-habits-is-overrated-heres); [UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)
- Clear's identity-based habits name three layers of change: outcomes, processes, and identity ("the third and deepest layer"). His two steps: "Decide the type of person you want to be" and "Prove it to yourself with small wins." — [jamesclear.com/identity-based-habits](https://jamesclear.com/identity-based-habits)
- [WEAK/POPULAR, theory-backed] BJ Fogg: "**B=MAP** — Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt come together at the same time." When a behavior doesn't happen, at least one element is missing. — [behaviormodel.org](https://behaviormodel.org/). The Tiny Habits recipe is "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behavior]," followed immediately by a celebration. The anchor is an existing behavior that already happens reliably, such as pouring coffee. The tiny behavior keeps ability high so motivation matters less, e.g. floss one tooth or do two push-ups. — [The Meaning Movement](https://themeaningmovement.com/tiny-habits). Fogg calls the positive feeling from celebrating "**Shine**". — [Coachway](https://coachway.io/articles/bj-fogg-tiny-habits-for-coaches/)

### Inferences
- **Why habits fail, synthesized:** (1) The cue is vague or doesn't recur at the habit's frequency (Clear). (2) The behavior is too big for low-motivation days (Fogg's ability factor; the two-minute rule). (3) Performance is inconsistent: occasional misses are fine, chronic inconsistency isn't (Lally). (4) There's no plan for the predictable obstacle (Gollwitzer, WOOP). (5) People quit after a broken streak, an all-or-nothing reaction that apps amplify by emphasizing streaks (Silverman & Barasch; Sharif & Shu). (6) Expectations are unrealistic: "21 days" vs a reality of 2–6+ months (Lally, Singh, Buyalskaya).
- **Recommended habit-module features, ranked by evidence:**
  1. **If-then plan field required on every habit** [STRONG]: "When/after [specific anchor] at [place], I will [behavior]." Validate that the anchor is specific (a concrete event, not "in the morning") and recurs at the habit's frequency.
  2. **Obstacle step (WOOP) on habit creation and on goals** [STRONG]: "What in you is most likely to stop this?" → "If [obstacle], then I will [response]." Interactive, guided delivery (chat/AI coach) is plausibly closer to the stronger "experimenter interaction" condition (g = 0.465) than a static form (g = 0.277). That is an extrapolation, not tested in apps.
  3. **Progress recorded and visible** [STRONG]: one-tap logging right after the anchor, a visible calendar/heatmap, and optional accountability sharing with a co-founder or partner (public reporting increased effects in Harkin).
  4. **Streaks with forgiveness** [MODERATE]: default goal framed as "7/week with 2 reserve skips" rather than a rigid 7/7. A skip spent deliberately doesn't break the streak. Offer **streak repair** (e.g., do the minimum version within 24h, or a double session) rather than resetting to 0. Never show a big red "streak lost" screen. Show "X of last 30 days" consistency instead of, or alongside, consecutive-day counts.
  5. **"Never miss twice" recovery prompt** [heuristic, consistent with Lally and with Buyalskaya's time-since-last-occurrence predictor]: after one miss, the next-day prompt offers the **minimum version** ("2-minute version: open the CRM and log 1 contact").
  6. **Minimum/standard/stretch versions per habit** (Fogg tiny + Clear two-minute rule): logging the minimum counts as a "show-up".
  7. **Few active habits** (Clear: track one consistently rather than ten sporadically): cap new active habits at 1–3, with a "habit queue" for the rest.
  8. **Realistic timeline and graduation** [STRONG on variability]: show "habits typically take 2–5 months; yours may be quicker or slower". At day 30/60/90, ask a short automaticity check ("Do I do this without thinking?"). When it's high, "graduate" the habit to lighter tracking, which reduces the burden of tracking as a second habit.
  9. **Fresh-start nudges** [MODERATE]: suggest starting or restarting habits on Mondays, the 1st of the month, quarter starts and birthdays, and frame a restart as a "new chapter" after a lapse.
  10. **Celebration micro-moment** [POPULAR]: an instant satisfying animation or "shine" on logging.
- **Identity framing** (Clear) can be a habit-level field: "This habit is a vote for: 'I am a founder who follows up with every lead.'" This is plausible but not separately evidenced in what I found.
- Atomic Habits' "Four Laws of Behavior Change" (make it obvious / attractive / easy / satisfying, with inversions for breaking bad habits) come from the book. None of the jamesclear.com pages I fetched this session stated them, so cite them to the book directly. They map onto the features above: obvious = cue/stacking, attractive = temptation bundling, easy = two-minute rule, satisfying = tracking and celebration.

### Gaps
- I didn't retrieve a peer-reviewed RCT that tests streak-repair or "never miss twice" features inside a habit app. The evidence is from adjacent paradigms: Silverman & Barasch, Sharif & Shu.
- Exact effect sizes for Tiny Habits' celebration step: I found no peer-reviewed test of "celebration" isolated from the other components.
- Lally's missed-day coding and analysis details: full text not retrieved.
- Whether habit stacking specifically (vs implementation intentions generally) has its own evidence base. It is functionally an implementation intention with a behavioral cue, but I found no separate meta-analysis.
- The Milkman et al. StepUp gym megastudy (2021), which tested many nudges including missed-workout recovery bonuses, wasn't retrieved this session. It's worth checking for direct evidence on recovery incentives.

## 2. How should routines (morning, start-up, shutdown, evening, weekly reset) be structured as checklists vs habits? What do well-known shutdown rituals contain?

### Takeaway
Treat a **routine as a checklist that one strong anchor triggers**, a "habit stack". Each item can mature into its own habit. Routines that open or close the workday matter most for an overloaded founder because they capture open loops, pick the day's priorities and create psychological detachment. Cal Newport's shutdown ritual is the canonical template. The evidence behind it comes from attention-residue research (unfinished tasks keep pulling attention) and recovery research (detachment reduces exhaustion).

### Cited Findings
- **Cal Newport's work shutdown ritual**, done at the end of each workday:
  1. Update master task lists: move new items from collection bins (a desktop text file, a notebook) into master lists, and give urgent items near-term due dates.
  2. Review the task lists in full, which builds trust that nothing will be forgotten.
  3. Review the calendar for the **next two weeks**.
  4. Review the weekly plan: annotate what got done and revise the remaining days.
  5. Close the computer and say "**schedule shutdown, complete**."

  If a work worry arises after shutdown, the rule is to reason: "I said the termination phrase; I wouldn't have said it if I hadn't checked all my tasks, calendar and weekly plan; therefore there is no need to worry." Don't think through the specific worry. — [Cal Newport, 2009](https://www.calnewport.com/blog/2009/06/08/drastically-reduce-stress-with-a-work-shutdown-ritual/)
- [MODERATE] Attention residue (Leroy 2009, *OBHDP*): when people switch tasks, "part of our attention often stays with the prior task (Task A) instead of fully transferring." People struggle to disengage from *unfinished* work, and performance on the next task suffers. Follow-up work (Leroy & Glomb 2018, *Organization Science*) shows a "**ready-to-resume plan**" mitigates residue from interrupted tasks: a quick note on where you are and what you'll do next. — [Sophie Leroy, UW Bothell](https://www.uwb.edu/business/faculty/sophie-leroy/attention-residue)
- [STRONG, correlational] Recovery experiences (Sonnentag & Fritz 2007): four separable off-job recovery experiences, namely **psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, and control**, validated across samples totaling 930 people. — [Univ. Konstanz](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/901f4299-e3f5-46af-99cb-a27bdd4bca71). A later meta-analysis covering k = 316 samples and N = 99,329 found that **relaxation and mastery** predicted both job and personal outcomes, while **detachment mainly reduced exhaustion and negative affect**. Control had negligible incremental effects. Diary studies showed smaller effects than cross-sectional ones. All of this is correlational. — [SJSU ScholarWorks record](https://scholarworks.sjsu.edu/faculty_rsca/3599)
- Habit stacking can chain a whole routine. Clear's example: "after finishing dinner, put the plate in the dishwasher, then wipe the counter, then set out tomorrow's coffee mug." New behaviors can be inserted mid-routine. — [jamesclear.com/habit-stacking](https://jamesclear.com/habit-stacking)
- Lally's participants performed behaviors "in the same context" daily. Context stability is part of what makes repetition become automatic. — [UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)
- The checklist format lets you resume a review where you left off. Tiago Forte does his weekly review in short sessions over 2–3 days. He frames it as a "scheduled departure from chaos back into order." — [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/the-weekly-review-is-an-operating-system/); [Forte, "The Design of a Weekly Review"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/17331631-the-design-of-a-weekly-review?tab=book)
- The GTD Weekly Review (Get Clear / Get Current / Get Creative) is the canonical *weekly reset* checklist; full contents are in §3. — [GTD checklist PDF](https://gettingthingsdone.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/Weekly_Review_Checklist.pdf)

### Inferences
**Design principle: habits vs checklists**
- A **habit** is a single behavior that becomes automatic. The app tracks it for streaks and automaticity and keeps the count small (1–3 active).
- A **routine** is an ordered checklist of 3–8 steps that a single anchor triggers ("When I open my laptop at the desk…"). Track it as *one* unit: completed, partial, or minimum. Individual steps stay optional and skippable. Each routine also has a **minimum version** of 1–2 items for bad days.
- Routines should be **time-boxed** (start-up ≤15 min, shutdown ≤10 min). This keeps the cost below the threshold where overloaded users skip them.
- The app should allow promoting a routine step to a tracked habit, and graduating a habit into a routine step once it's automatic.

**Proposed routine templates.** These are synthesized from the sources above. Items marked * are drawn from a named source; unmarked items are design suggestions.

*Morning routine (personal, pre-work), minimum version = items 1–2*
1. Hydrate, and get daylight or movement if possible (simple anchor-driven habits).
2. 1-tap check-in: sleep hours/quality, energy 1–5, mood 1–5 (see §4).
3. Optional: 3-minute journal ("What would make today a good day?" / intention). See §5 for the evidence, which is modest.
4. No inbox until the start-up routine is done. This is a design suggestion that protects the planning step; it's not evidence-tested.

*Work start-up routine (≤15 min), minimum version = items 2 and 4*
1. Glance at today's calendar and the weekly plan / weekly Big 3.*
2. Pick **today's Top 3 outcomes**: at least one delivery, and one revenue-generating (sales/marketing) item if this is a sales-heavy week.
3. Assign each Top-3 item to a calendar block matched to energy (deep work in the peak-energy window; admin and sales calls in lower-energy windows, see §4).
4. For each focus block, write an if-then plan: "If I get pinged by a client during the block, then I will note it and reply at the next break."*
5. Check the "waiting for" list and today's follow-ups.*

*Work shutdown routine (≤10 min), modeled on Newport*
1. Capture: empty quick-capture inbox items into the task system.*
2. Review open tasks and move/date anything urgent.*
3. Review the calendar for the next 2 weeks.*
4. Mark today's Top 3 as done/partial/moved, and update the weekly plan.*
5. Write a "**ready-to-resume**" note for any unfinished deep-work task: where I stopped, the next concrete step.*
6. Log a 1-line "progress today" entry (Amabile's progress principle, §5).
7. Tap "**Shutdown complete**." The app then mutes work notifications, which supports psychological detachment.*

*Evening routine (personal), minimum version = item 3*
1. Set out tomorrow's anchors, e.g. gym bag or notebook (Clear-style environment cueing).
2. 2-minute reflection or gratitude (optional, §5).
3. Wind-down cutoff for screens and work, aimed at protecting sleep (§4, §6).
4. A relaxation or mastery activity: reading, hobby or exercise. These are the recovery experiences most associated with good outcomes.*

*Weekly reset (60–90 min, Friday afternoon or Sunday evening):* see the merged weekly review template in §3.

### Gaps
- I didn't retrieve the primary Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) study on how making a plan reduces intrusive thoughts about unfinished goals (the "Zeigarnik" rationale often attached to shutdown rituals). Newport's original 2009 post doesn't cite it. It should be verified before being cited.
- No controlled study tests Newport's specific shutdown ritual or "shutdown complete" phrase. Its support is indirect, via attention residue, recovery and implementation intentions.
- No peer-reviewed evidence was found for specific "morning routine" contents (e.g., cold showers, 5 a.m. waking) improving founder performance. Treat them as personal preference.

## 3. Best-practice templates for daily planning, daily reflection/journaling, weekly, monthly, quarterly and annual reviews

### Takeaway
The proven practitioner systems agree on one structure: **look back (wins, misses, what worked), get current (inboxes, calendar, lists), score execution, then commit to a small number of priorities (Big 3)**. Weekly is the critical cadence. GTD's weekly review, the Full Focus Weekly Preview and the 12 Week Year scorecard all center on it. Annual reviews add a "what energized vs drained me" audit (Ferriss) and goal-setting by life category (Guillebeau). Research supports the active ingredients: short daily reflection improves learning (Di Stefano et al.), noticing progress drives motivation (Amabile), and recorded, visible progress tracking improves attainment (Harkin).

### Cited Findings

**Daily**
- [MODERATE] Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano & Staats, "Learning by Thinking". In a field study with trainees at an outsourcing/call-center firm, employees who spent **the last 15 minutes of each training day writing and reflecting on what they had learned did 23% better on the final training test**. The reflection group got a basic journal prompt to reflect on the day. — [HEC Paris](https://www.hec.edu/en/knowledge/instants/power-reflection-work-insight-hec-professor-giada-di-stefano); [CPREE review](https://cpree.uw.edu/a-review-of-learning-by-thinking-how-reflection-aids-performance). Sources conflict on the field site ("international call center" vs "Indian BPO, Wipro"), and some summaries loosely restate the 23% as "productivity". A later version reports **10 experiments (N = 4,340)**: reflection is most beneficial **early in the learning curve**, once enough experience has accumulated to reflect on. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2414478)
- [MODERATE] Amabile & Kramer's *Progress Principle* (2011) is based on about 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 knowledge workers at 7 companies. "Of all the events that characterized the best inner work life days, by far the most prominent was making progress," and setbacks were the most prominent on the worst days. **Setbacks had 2–3× stronger effects than progress.** Small wins were nearly as effective as major breakthroughs. Tangible incentives barely registered. — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html)

**Weekly**
- **GTD Weekly Review** (David Allen) has three phases. **Get Clear**: gather loose papers and materials into the in-tray; process all outstanding paper, journal and meeting notes, voicemails and email; empty your head. **Get Current**: review action lists and mark off completed items; review the previous calendar for follow-ups and the upcoming calendar for preparation; review the Waiting For list; review project lists. **Get Creative**: review Someday/Maybe and capture new ideas. Friday afternoon is a common time. — [GTD Weekly Review Checklist PDF](https://gettingthingsdone.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/Weekly_Review_Checklist.pdf); [FacileThings GTD dictionary](https://www.facilethings.com/gtd-dictionary/en/weekly-review)
- **Full Focus Weekly Preview** (Michael Hyatt):
  1. List your biggest wins from the past week ("In a word: confidence").
  2. Write the weekly Big 3 and the % completed for each, with partial credit allowed (75%, 50%, 100%).
  3. Reflect on what worked, what didn't, and what to keep / improve / start / stop.
  4. Do a list sweep of deferred tasks.
  5. Plan the next week's Big 3.

  Do it Friday afternoon, Sunday evening or first thing Monday, and allow about 1 hour. — [Full Focus Weekly Preview PDF](https://fullfocusplanner.com/wp-content/themes/fullfocusplanner-v6/files/quickstart/Weekly%20Preview%20Pages.pdf). One practitioner writes 3–10 wins and marks each Big 3 item complete / cancelled / carried forward. — [Scott Monday](https://scottmonday.substack.com/p/weekly-preview)
- **12 Week Year** (Moran & Lennington). The year becomes 12-week "years". Each week has a plan of lead-indicator tactics, and the weekly **execution score** is the % of planned tactics completed. It measures *actions*, not results, and the benchmark is **≥85%**. The weekly accountability meeting (WAM) is short, about 15–20 min: last week's score, the coming week's plan, what needs to change. — [monday.com summary](https://monday.com/blog/productivity/12-week-year-time-management-system/). A secondary concept page gives tiers (70–84% "good, but leaving results on the table"; <70% "execution breakdown"), but these tiers aren't clearly from the book. — [dsebastien concept note](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/12-week-year/)
- **Tiago Forte's weekly review**:
  1. Clear email inbox, done first so later decisions use the latest information. Route each message to the calendar, task manager, reference store or read-later.
  2. Check the calendar from 2 weeks back to 4 weeks ahead.
  3. Clear the physical inbox and notebook.
  4. Clear the computer desktop and downloads, filing with PARA.
  5. (Optional linked habit) Review spending transactions.
  6. Process the notes inbox.
  7. Prioritize and file new open loops (next action, priority, project/area).
  8. Review Waiting For.
  9. Choose today's tasks: urgency first, then importance from the 2–3 projects in focus.

  It takes 15–30 min. — [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/the-weekly-review-is-an-operating-system/). He later simplified it to a five-item list: **email, calendar, desktop/downloads, notes, tasks**. — [Forte, "The Design of a Weekly Review"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/17331631-the-design-of-a-weekly-review?tab=book)
- Progress monitoring works better when it's **physically recorded** and **reported to others**. — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)
- Monday or week-start is a natural fresh-start landmark for recommitting. — [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)

**Quarterly**
- Peter Wildeford's quarterly review and plan template draws on Alex Vermeer/Gustin-style annual reviews, the Quarterly Productivity Planner and Ben Todd's annual review. Its first step looks back at the prior quarter: key successes, key mistakes, progress on prior goals. The plan follows. — [Peter Wildeford](https://peterwildeford.substack.com/p/my-template-for-a-quarterly-review)
- Guillebeau checks goals monthly and holds longer quarterly reviews. — [Chris Guillebeau](https://chrisguillebeau.com/how-to-conduct-your-own-annual-review/)
- The 12 Week Year treats each 12-week period as a full planning cycle, which is effectively a quarterly reset. — [monday.com summary](https://monday.com/blog/productivity/12-week-year-time-management-system/)

**Annual**
- **Chris Guillebeau's annual review**:
  1. Spend the better part of a week in late December, in pieces.
  2. Ask "What went well this year?" and "What did not go well this year?", aiming for **6–8 answers each** and focusing on things you could control.
  3. Review last year's goals in a "Final Review" column. **About 80% achieved is healthy**; 100% may mean goals were too low.
  4. Set **3–5 measurable goals** in each of 11 categories: Writing, Business, Friends & Family, Service, Travel, Spiritual, Health, Learning, Financial-Earning, Financial-Giving, Financial-Saving.
  5. Add an "Actions Required" column for each goal.
  6. Keep a list of optional extras.
  7. Choose a yearly theme and a one-paragraph statement *last*.
  8. Keep a metrics tab compared across years.
  9. Do monthly check-ins and quarterly reviews.
  10. Bring in task tools only after high-level planning is done.

  — [chrisguillebeau.com](https://chrisguillebeau.com/how-to-conduct-your-own-annual-review/)
- **Tim Ferriss's Past Year Review (PYR)**:
  1. Make two columns, POSITIVE and NEGATIVE.
  2. Go through last year's calendar **week by week** and note the people, activities or commitments that triggered peak positive or negative emotions.
  3. Ask "What 20% of each column produced the most reliable or powerful peaks?"
  4. Put the positives on the calendar now ("It's not real until it's in the calendar"): book and prepay.
  5. Turn the negatives into a visible "**Not-To-Do list**", and don't schedule them out of obligation, guilt or FOMO.

  It takes about 30–60 min. — [tim.blog, 2018](https://tim.blog/2018/12/28/past-year-review/)
- Tiago Forte's annual review book *Life in Perspective* uses an "ARC" method: **Appreciate the past, Reflect on the present, Create the future**. — [Forte via Goodreads blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/21007203-exposing-opportunity-with-an-annual-review)

### Inferences
**Proposed review templates for the app.** These synthesize the sources above, with source tags in brackets. Keep each one short enough to finish.

*A. Daily plan (start-up, 5–10 min)*
- Today's Top 3 outcomes [Full Focus "Big 3" scaled to daily]. Tag each as Delivery / Sales / Marketing / Ops / Personal, so the founder sees allocation across roles.
- Which Top-3 item moves this week's Big 3 or 12-week goal? [12WY]
- Energy forecast (H/M/L), then the app proposes block placement (§4).
- If-then plan for the biggest expected distraction [Gollwitzer].
- Calendar sanity check: meetings + focus blocks ≤ available hours, with a 20–30% buffer (design heuristic, not evidence-based).

*B. Daily reflection / shutdown journal (≤5 min; prompts rotate, max 3 shown)*
- "What progress did I make today on meaningful work?" (1 line) [Amabile]
- "What did I learn today / what would I do differently?" [Di Stefano: 15-min end-of-day reflection]
- "What set me back, and is there a next step to unblock it?" [Amabile: setbacks are 2–3× stronger, so address them explicitly]
- Optional: "One thing I'm grateful for" [small effect, §5]
- Auto-captured: Top-3 completion (done/partial/moved), focus minutes, energy/mood ratings.

*C. Weekly review (60–90 min; Fri PM or Sun PM; resumable checklist)* [GTD + Full Focus + 12WY + Forte]
1. **Celebrate**: list 3–10 wins (the app pre-fills them from completed tasks and progress notes). [Full Focus; Amabile]
2. **Score**: Big 3 completion % with partial credit [Full Focus], plus an execution score = completed planned tactics ÷ planned tactics, with an 85% target [12WY]. Habit consistency is shown as "X/7 incl. reserves".
3. **Reflect**: What worked? What didn't? Keep / Improve / Start / Stop. [Full Focus]
4. **Get clear**: empty inboxes (email, notes, quick-capture, paper), and clear the desktop/downloads. [GTD; Forte]
5. **Get current**: calendar 2 weeks back (follow-ups) and 4 weeks ahead (prep) [Forte]; Waiting For [GTD]; project list, with one next action per active project [GTD]; **pipeline check** (founder-specific: leads to follow up, proposals out); delivery commitments due.
6. **Get creative**: Someday/Maybe and new ideas. [GTD]
7. **Energy and wellbeing look-back**: weekly averages of sleep, energy and mood; hours worked; days with a full shutdown; burnout pulse (§6).
8. **Plan**: next week's Big 3 (≤3) and the planned tactics list (12WY lead indicators); block deep-work time in the calendar now; WOOP the riskiest Big-3 item [Oettingen].
9. **Share** (optional): send the score and Big 3 to a co-founder or accountability partner [Harkin: public reporting; 12WY WAM].

*D. Monthly review (30–45 min, 1st of month, a fresh-start landmark)*
- Read the 4 weekly reviews and note recurring wins and recurring blockers.
- Goal check: progress on quarterly / 12-week goals, on track / at risk / off track [Guillebeau: monthly check-ins].
- Habits: which to graduate (automatic), which to shrink (repeated minimum-only), which to drop, and which single new habit to start this month (fresh start).
- Time audit: hours by role (delivery / sales / marketing / admin) vs intended allocation.
- Energy patterns: best and worst days and what preceded them, e.g. sleep or meeting load (§4).
- Finances: personal and business cash quick-look (optional module).
- "One thing to stop doing next month."

*E. Quarterly review (2–3 h, or a 12-week cycle end)* [12WY, Wildeford, Guillebeau]
- Look back: key successes, key mistakes, progress on each goal (score %), average weekly execution score. [Wildeford; 12WY]
- Life-area scan (rate 1–10): Health, Relationships/Family, Business-Delivery, Business-Sales/Marketing, Finances, Learning, Fun/Recovery. This is an adaptation of Guillebeau's categories and a design suggestion.
- Burnout check: exhaustion / cynicism / efficacy trend (§6).
- Set the next 12-week goals: 1–3 per area max, each with lead-indicator tactics and a WOOP.
- "Not-to-do list" refresh [Ferriss].

*F. Annual review (half-day to a few sessions in late Dec / early Jan, or the company's fiscal year end)*
- **Past Year Review**: scroll the year's calendar week by week, auto-populated from the app; tag positive and negative peaks; identify the top 20% of each. Positives get scheduled now, negatives go on the Not-To-Do list. [Ferriss]
- **What went well / what didn't**, 6–8 each, about things within your control. [Guillebeau]
- Goal scorecard: % achieved, with about 80% considered healthy. [Guillebeau]
- **Appreciate / Reflect / Create**. [Forte ARC]
- Set 3–5 measurable goals per chosen category, with an actions column. [Guillebeau]
- Choose a theme for the year last. [Guillebeau]
- Year-over-year metrics, e.g. revenue, clients, hours worked, average sleep, workouts, vacation days taken. [Guillebeau's metrics tab]

### Gaps
- I found no Tiago Forte-authored monthly or quarterly review question set. Only the weekly checklist and the annual ARC method were verified.
- I found no peer-reviewed RCT comparing weekly review systems (GTD vs 12WY vs Full Focus). Their value rests on practitioner reports, plus indirect evidence from progress monitoring, implementation intentions and reflection.
- The 12 Week Year's 85% threshold appears to be the authors' practitioner claim; I found no published data behind it.
- The Ferriss PYR page used here is the 2018 post; later versions (e.g., 2021, and 2025 "Step 3") exist and were not checked for changes.

## 4. How should energy, mood, sleep and health be tracked lightly so they inform planning (incl. focus blocks, Pomodoro, ultradian rhythms, chronotypes)?

### Takeaway
"Manage energy, not time" is a useful product principle. But the popular specifics are weakly evidenced: strict 90-minute ultradian cycles are contested, chronotype "peak hours" are inconsistent in younger adults, and Pomodoro is not clearly superior. The robust findings are these. Structured breaks reduce fatigue and boost vigor modestly. Sleep loss degrades founders' judgment. Task switching leaves attention residue. Recovery experiences (relaxation, mastery, detachment) are associated with lower exhaustion. So the app should collect **minimal self-reports** (sleep, energy, mood, 1–5 taps), learn **the individual's** patterns over weeks, and use them to suggest block placement, not impose a universal schedule.

### Cited Findings

**Energy management frameworks**
- [WEAK/POPULAR] Schwartz & McCarthy, "Manage Your Energy, Not Your Time" (HBR, Oct 2007; the same school as Loehr & Schwartz's *The Power of Full Engagement*). Energy has four renewable sources: **body (physical), emotions, mind, spirit**. Each gets its own rituals: intermittent breaks for physical energy, shifting perspective for emotional energy, avoiding constant tech distractions for mental energy, and meaningful or purposeful activities for spiritual energy. In a Wachovia pilot, participants reportedly outperformed controls on financial metrics such as loan revenue and reported gains in relationships, productivity and satisfaction. No sample sizes or statistics are available; it is an authors' internal pilot. The rituals reportedly succeed only with leadership support. — [Physician Leaders summary](https://www.physicianleaders.org/articles/manage-your-energy-not-your-time); [HBR store listing](https://store.hbr.org/product/manage-your-energy-not-your-time/R0710B)

**Ultradian rhythms**
- [CONTESTED] Kleitman proposed a ~90-minute (80–120 min) Basic Rest–Activity Cycle (BRAC) operating during waking as well as sleep. Wikipedia says more recent analyses support waking ultradian cycles (in focus and reaction time) but that these operate via different mechanisms from the nightly REM–NREM cycle. I couldn't verify the meta-analysis behind that claim. — [Wikipedia: BRAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_rest%E2%80%93activity_cycle)
- A Nature paper reported the REM cycle is sleep-dependent rather than an expression of a continuous activity-independent rhythm, which undercuts the waking extension. Early critics titled a paper "There's no basic rest-activity cycle" (Kripke et al., 1977, via secondary source). Waking-performance studies are mixed: Klein & Armitage (1979) found 60–100 min oscillations, while Neubauer & Freudenthaler (1995) reportedly found none. — [Nature PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/265360a0.pdf); [Wikipedia: BRAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_rest%E2%80%93activity_cycle)

**Chronotype / time-of-day**
- [MODERATE] Chauhan et al. (2025, *Chronobiology International* 42(4):463–499), a systematic review of 65 studies. **More than 80% found no main effect of chronotype on cognition.** A "synchrony effect" (better performance at one's optimal time of day) appeared in **45% (29/64) of studies of adults aged 18–45**, mostly on attention, inhibition and memory tasks, and in **83% (10/12) of studies of older adults**. Task complexity and lighting moderate it. — [Kingston University record](https://researchinnovation.kingston.ac.uk/en/publications/chronotype-and-synchrony-effects-in-human-cognitive-performance-a/)
- For gym habit formation, time of day didn't matter. — [Buyalskaya et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10151500)

**Breaks, Pomodoro and focus blocks**
- [STRONG-ish] Albulescu et al. (2022, *PLOS ONE*), a meta-analysis of micro-breaks (≤10 min) covering 22 samples (N = 2,335). Micro-breaks boosted **vigor (d = .36)** and reduced **fatigue (d = .35)**. The overall **performance effect was non-significant (d = .16)** and appeared only for **less cognitively demanding tasks**. Longer breaks were associated with larger performance gains, and highly depleting tasks may need breaks over 10 min. — [DOAJ / PLOS ONE](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)
- [MODERATE] Biwer et al. (2023) compared self-regulated breaks, "Pomodoro" (24-min work / 6-min break, n = 25) and short systematic breaks (12/3, n = 27) in 87 university students. Self-regulated breakers had **longer sessions and breaks, with higher fatigue and distractedness and lower concentration and motivation**. There was **no difference in mental effort or task completion**. Systematic breaks gave mood benefits, plus efficiency benefits from similar completion in less time. — [PubMed 36859717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)
- [MODERATE, conflicting] A 2025 *Behavioral Sciences* study comparing Pomodoro, Flowtime and self-regulated breaks found Pomodoro led to a faster rise in fatigue and Pomodoro and Flowtime to a faster drop in motivation. But there were **no overall differences** in fatigue, motivation, productivity, task completion or flow. — [MDPI Behav. Sci. 15(7):861](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/7/861)
- [MODERATE] Attention residue: switching away from *unfinished* tasks degrades performance on the next task. A ready-to-resume plan mitigates this. — [Leroy, UW Bothell](https://www.uwb.edu/business/faculty/sophie-leroy/attention-residue)

**Sleep and founders**
- [MODERATE] Gish, Wagner, Grégoire & Barnes (2019, *Journal of Business Venturing* 34(6)) studied more than 700 entrepreneurs evaluating expert-ranked pitches. **Less sleep led to less accurate beliefs about a new venture idea's commercial potential.** — [UCF News](https://www.ucf.edu/news/study-sleep-is-essential-for-business-leaders-seeking-next-successful-venture/); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/entthe/v43y2019i3p582-610.html)
- A related 2021 paper (Gish, Gunia, Mensmann, *Entrepreneurship Theory & Practice*) found sleep problems can push people toward entrepreneurial intentions while still undermining performance. — [Warwick Business School](https://www.wbs.ac.uk/news/why-sleep-problems-encourage-entrepreneurial-intentions/)

**Tracking itself**
- Recording progress physically, and reporting it, strengthens the effect of monitoring. — [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)
- Tracking can become a burden because it is "two habits", so track few things consistently. — [James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/habit-tracker)

### Inferences
**Light-touch tracking schema** (target: under 20 seconds/day)
- *Morning check-in (1 screen):* sleep hours (auto from a wearable if connected, else a quick picker), sleep quality 1–5, energy 1–5, mood 1–5. Optional tags: sick, travel, alcohol, late meeting, exercise yesterday.
- *Optional mid-day/afternoon ping:* energy 1–5, a single tap. This builds the person's own time-of-day energy curve. Better than assuming a chronotype, since >80% of studies found no chronotype main effect and synchrony effects are inconsistent in adults under 45.
- *Shutdown:* hours worked (auto from focus/calendar if possible), focus minutes, "Did I fully shut down?" yes/no.
- *Weekly:* exercise sessions, social/recovery activities (relaxation/mastery), days off.

**Energy-informed planning rules** (design heuristics grounded in the findings above)
- Tag tasks with **energy demand** (High = deep/creative work such as product delivery, proposal writing or strategy; Medium = sales calls, content; Low = admin, email, invoicing) and **context** (deep / shallow / people).
- After about 2–3 weeks of check-ins, compute the personal peak window (the hours with the highest average energy) and suggest high-demand tasks there. Before enough data exists, default to "first block after start-up".
- If morning energy ≤2 or sleep is under 6h, the app proposes a **"low-energy day plan"**: shrink to Top 1, move high-demand tasks, protect a break schedule, and suggest minimum versions of habits. This is supported indirectly by Gish (sleep→judgment) and Sharif & Shu (slack sustains persistence).
- **Focus blocks:** default 50–90 min of single-tasking with notifications muted, followed by a real break. Offer Pomodoro (25/5) and "flowtime" (work until a natural stop, then a proportional break) as user options rather than prescribing one; the evidence doesn't favor either strongly. Systematic breaks reduce fatigue versus self-regulated breaks that drift long (Biwer). For depleting work, suggest breaks over 10 min (Albulescu).
- Batch shallow work and sales outreach into blocks to reduce switching (attention residue). Before ending or interrupting a deep block, prompt a 1-line "ready-to-resume" note.
- Don't market "90-minute ultradian cycles" as science. At most offer "90-minute focus block" as a preset.

**Insights the app can surface** (correlations within the person's own data, labeled as such)
- "Your energy averages 1.2 points lower after nights under 6.5h."
- "Weeks with 3+ workouts: average mood 3.9 vs 3.1."
- "Your best progress days started with a deep-work block before 11:00."

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous evidence that tracking mood or energy *by itself* improves outcomes for healthy users. The benefit here is inferred from progress-monitoring and self-monitoring literatures (Harkin) and from personalization logic.
- The Loehr & Schwartz *Power of Full Engagement* book claims (e.g., the "full engagement" ritual model and athlete-derived oscillation principle) weren't verified against the primary text this session. Only the 2007 HBR article summary was retrieved.
- I found no quantitative pooled effect size for chronotype synchrony effects.
- No peer-reviewed data was retrieved on the optimal focus-block length for knowledge work. The 50–90 min defaults are design heuristics.

## 5. What evidence exists on journaling, gratitude and reflection for performance and wellbeing?

### Takeaway
The evidence is real but **modest**, and the strongest support is for **work-focused reflection**, not generic gratitude. Reflection on what one learned improves performance, especially early on a learning curve (Di Stefano et al.). Noticing daily progress is the top driver of positive work motivation (Amabile). Expressive writing has a small but significant average effect (r ≈ .075, d ≈ 0.15). Gratitude interventions produce only small reductions in depression and anxiety (g ≈ −0.29), possibly partly placebo. Journaling should be quick, optional and structured around progress, lessons and setbacks, with gratitude as an optional add-on, not the core.

### Cited Findings
- [STRONG, small effect] Frattaroli (2006, *Psychological Bulletin*), "Experimental Disclosure and Its Moderators". A meta-analysis of **146 randomized studies** of expressive writing/disclosure found a positive, significant average **r = .075** (≈ d = 0.15), with benefits for psychological health, self-reported physical health and general functioning, and wide variability across studies. — [Frattaroli 2006 PDF (gwern.net)](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/2006-frattaroli.pdf); [PMC3830620 (later paper citing it)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3830620)
- [STRONG, small effect] Cregg & Cheavens (2021, *Journal of Happiness Studies* 22), a meta-analysis of gratitude interventions covering **27 studies (N = 3,675)**. It found a **small effect on depression and anxiety symptoms (g ≈ −0.29 at post-test)**, slightly larger vs waitlist than vs active controls, possibly attributable to placebo. The authors recommended that people seeking to reduce depression or anxiety use methods with stronger evidence. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jhappi/v22y2021i1d10.1007_s10902-020-00236-6.html); [Mad in America summary](https://www.madinamerica.com/2020/03/gratitude-interventions-insufficient-reduce-anxiety-depression/)
- [MODERATE] 15 minutes of end-of-day written reflection produced 23% better final-test performance in a field training setting. Across 10 experiments (N = 4,340), reflection helps most early in learning, once there is experience to reflect on. — [HEC Paris](https://www.hec.edu/en/knowledge/instants/power-reflection-work-insight-hec-professor-giada-di-stefano); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2414478)
- [MODERATE] Progress Principle: across about 12,000 diary entries, making progress in meaningful work was the most prominent feature of the best days. Setbacks hit 2–3× harder. Small wins counted nearly as much as big ones. "On days when employees sensed they made headway in meaningful work, their emotions were more positive, their drive to perform peaked." — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html)
- [STRONG] Thinking through obstacles in a structured way (mental contrasting + if-then) improves goal attainment, g = 0.336. This is a reflective practice with stronger evidence than gratitude. — [Wang et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8149892/)
- Full Focus starts its weekly review with wins, explicitly to build confidence. — [Full Focus PDF](https://fullfocusplanner.com/wp-content/themes/fullfocusplanner-v6/files/quickstart/Weekly%20Preview%20Pages.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The journaling module should prioritize, in order:**
  1. **Progress log** (1 line/day, auto-suggested from completed tasks), feeding weekly "wins" [Amabile; Full Focus].
  2. **Lesson / do-differently** prompt at shutdown [Di Stefano].
  3. **Setback + next step** prompt. It converts rumination into an action, which matters because setbacks weigh 2–3× more [Amabile].
  4. **WOOP** for tomorrow's or this week's hardest item [Wang et al.].
  5. **Gratitude** as an optional toggle, positioned as a pleasant ritual, not as treatment [Cregg & Cheavens].
  6. **Expressive writing** "brain dump" mode for stressful periods: private, unstructured, no AI summarization by default [Frattaroli: small effect].
- Keep entries short (≤3 prompts, ≤5 min). The evidence base used short daily reflection (15 min max) and doesn't support long daily journaling as necessary.
- AI can add value by surfacing patterns across entries, such as recurring blockers or energy drains. That is an unvalidated feature and should be framed as "observations", not diagnoses.
- Don't make claims like "gratitude journaling reduces depression" in marketing copy. The evidence shows small effects that may be placebo.

### Gaps
- The Pennebaker expressive-writing protocol details (typically 15–20 min on 3–4 consecutive days writing about emotional experiences) weren't verified from a primary source this session.
- I found no studies specifically on founders or entrepreneurs using reflective journaling and business outcomes.
- I didn't check Frattaroli's moderators, e.g. dose, at-home vs lab, or which populations benefited most.
- I didn't retrieve evidence on "three good things" or "best possible self" exercises separately from the gratitude meta-analysis.

## 6. What warning signs and practices around founder burnout and overwork should the system build in?

### Takeaway
Founders face elevated mental-health risk: 72% of surveyed entrepreneurs reported mental-health concerns directly or via family history. Long hours of 55 or more per week carry well-documented cardiovascular risk. Sleep loss degrades founders' decision quality. Burnout is formally defined (ICD-11) by three dimensions: **exhaustion, mental distance/cynicism, and reduced professional efficacy**. A life OS should passively watch for leading indicators (hours, sleep, days off, shutdown completion, falling mood/energy, missed recovery) and run a short periodic burnout pulse on the three dimensions. When thresholds are crossed it should respond with *protective defaults*, not more productivity pressure, and point to professional help when signals are severe.

### Cited Findings
- [MODERATE] Freeman, Staudenmaier, Zisser & Andresen (2019, *Small Business Economics* 53:323–342) surveyed 242 entrepreneurs and 93 comparison participants. **72% of entrepreneurs** reported mental-health differences affecting them directly or indirectly: 49% from personal history, plus 23% from family history among otherwise asymptomatic founders. Entrepreneurs reported higher rates than comparison participants of **depression (30%), ADHD (29%), substance use (12%), and bipolar disorder (11%)**. These are prevalence rates in a convenience sample, not relative risks, and Freeman has said larger samples are needed. Many secondary sources garble these numbers. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs11187-018-0059-8); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/sbusec/v53y2019i2d10.1007_s11187-018-0059-8.html)
- [STRONG, definitional] WHO ICD-11 describes burnout as a syndrome resulting from **chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed**, with three dimensions: (1) energy depletion or exhaustion; (2) increased mental distance from one's job, or feelings of negativism or cynicism about it; (3) reduced professional efficacy. It is classified as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition, and applies specifically to the work context. — [KUOW/NPR](https://www.kuow.org/stories/who-redefines-burnout-as-a-syndrome-linked-to-chronic-stress-at-work); [WGBH](https://wgbh.org/news/national/2019-05-28/who-redefines-burnout-as-a-syndrome-linked-to-chronic-stress-at-work)
- [STRONG] WHO/ILO (2021) systematic analysis across 194 countries: working **≥55 hours/week**, compared with 35–40, is associated with higher risks of ischemic heart disease and stroke. It estimated **745,194 deaths** and 23.3M DALYs attributable in 2016. — [CDC Stacks record](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/230297)
- [MODERATE] Less sleep impairs entrepreneurs' evaluation of venture ideas. — [UCF News on Gish et al. 2019](https://www.ucf.edu/news/study-sleep-is-essential-for-business-leaders-seeking-next-successful-venture/)
- [STRONG, correlational] Psychological detachment from work mainly reduces exhaustion and negative affect. Relaxation and mastery experiences predict both wellbeing and job outcomes. — [SJSU ScholarWorks meta-analysis record](https://scholarworks.sjsu.edu/faculty_rsca/3599); [Sonnentag & Fritz 2007](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/901f4299-e3f5-46af-99cb-a27bdd4bca71)
- [MODERATE] Setbacks affect inner work life 2–3× more strongly than progress, so a run of setbacks (e.g., lost deals) is a mood risk worth noticing. — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html)
- [MODERATE] Self-blame amplifies the demotivating effect of a broken streak. Systems that frame lapses as personal failure may worsen disengagement. — [Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- Ferriss's "Not-To-Do list" removes the negative-peak people and commitments identified in an annual review, i.e. scheduling out the drains. — [tim.blog](https://tim.blog/2018/12/28/past-year-review/)

### Inferences
**Burnout early-warning signals.** The thresholds below are design proposals, not validated clinical cutoffs.

| Signal | Source of data | Proposed trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly hours worked | focus timer, calendar, shutdown log | ≥55 h in 2 of the last 3 weeks (WHO/ILO risk threshold) |
| Sleep | check-in / wearable | average under 6.5 h, or quality ≤2 on 4+ nights in a week |
| Days fully off | calendar / app usage | no full day off in 14 days |
| Shutdown completion | shutdown routine | completed on ≤2 of 5 workdays for 2 weeks (lack of detachment) |
| Energy and mood trend | daily check-in | 7-day rolling average down ≥1 point vs the 4-week baseline, or ≤2 for 5+ days |
| Recovery activities | weekly log | no relaxation/mastery activity logged in 2 weeks |
| Execution score collapse | weekly review | well below the user's norm 2+ weeks running, *with* high hours. Effort isn't converting, a possible efficacy decline |
| Cynicism language | optional, opt-in journal sentiment | sustained negativism about work. Use cautiously; privacy-sensitive |

**Monthly or quarterly burnout pulse** (3 items, 1–5 scale, mapped to the ICD-11 dimensions; *not* a validated instrument, so consider licensing a validated tool if clinical-grade measurement is needed)
1. Exhaustion: "How often did you feel drained of energy by work this month?"
2. Distance/cynicism: "How often did you feel detached from or negative about your work?"
3. Efficacy: "How confident are you that you're accomplishing worthwhile things at work?"

**Protective responses the system should take** (help the user decide; don't nag)
- Shift the plan to recovery: suggest a lighter week with Top 1 instead of Top 3, offer to move non-critical tasks, and propose blocking a full day off.
- Enforce the shutdown: after a set hour the app nudges "Shutdown complete?", hides work lists, and mutes work notifications in the evening.
- Habits fall back to minimum versions automatically, with reserves topped up. This avoids the broken-streak spiral during hard periods (Silverman & Barasch; Sharif & Shu).
- Self-compassionate copy: frame a lapse as "A missed day is normal; restart today" rather than "You broke your streak".
- Triage role overload: show the hours split across delivery, sales and marketing, and prompt "What could be delegated, automated, dropped or deferred?" (GTD-style get-current applied to commitments; Ferriss's Not-To-Do list).
- Social check: prompt a check-in with a co-founder, partner or peer group. Accountability sharing was positive in Harkin; social support as a burnout buffer was not verified here.
- **Escalation:** if severe or persistent low-mood signals appear, or the user reports thoughts of self-harm, show crisis and professional-help resources. Make clear the app isn't a clinical tool. Given Freeman's prevalence figures, founders are a higher-need population.

### Gaps
- I didn't retrieve longitudinal studies of *founder-specific* burnout prevalence after 2019, or of burnout interventions tested on founders.
- I didn't verify validated short burnout measures suitable for in-app use (e.g., single-item burnout measures, the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, the Burnout Assessment Tool), including their licensing and psychometrics.
- The social-support buffering effect for entrepreneurs wasn't retrieved this session.
- I didn't check whether automated warning-signal nudges in apps reduce burnout, or have unintended effects such as alarm fatigue or anxiety. I found no evidence either way.
