## What note types and linking structures actually work long term, and what are the common failure modes (collector's fallacy, over-organizing)?

### Takeaway
The approaches that last (Zettelkasten, evergreen notes, MOCs) agree on a few rules: a short-lived capture inbox, one idea per note written in your own words, links that say *why* two notes are related, and a small number of hub/index notes (structure notes, MOCs) made only when a topic gets messy. Deep folder or tag hierarchies are not the backbone. The main failure modes are collecting without processing (the collector's fallacy), links with no stated reason, copy-pasting, and note-making as busywork that never reaches a project or output.

### Cited Findings
**Zettelkasten (Luhmann; zettelkasten.de)**
- Definition: "A Zettelkasten is a personal tool for thinking and writing." It is hypertextual and puts connecting ahead of collecting — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)
- Atomicity: "The Principle of Atomicity is a processing direction in note-taking, aiming for one knowledge building block per note." It is presented as a guiding direction, not a hard rule — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)
- Each Zettel has three parts: a unique identifier, a body (in your own words), and references (the source, or blank for your own thoughts) — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)
- Writing in your own words is called the most important aspect of the body, because copying verbatim does not force understanding. Information is mostly "dead". Knowledge comes from adding context and relevance — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)
- Link context: every new Zettel should be placed in a relationship with another one, and "The explicit statement of the why is created knowledge." Links without an explanation create no knowledge, and a future reader can't tell why to follow them — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)
- Structure notes are meta-notes about other Zettel and how they relate. They work like a topic-specific table of contents and can hold sequential argument chains. Luhmann's hub notes were fast tracks pointing to many continuation points. His register (index) listed only a few central entry points per term, not tags — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)
- Unique ID options: Luhmann-style hierarchical (1, 1a, 1a1, which allows branching and interspersing), a timestamp, an arbitrary unique string, or the title. A fixed ID lets you rename titles without breaking links — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)
- Luhmann published 50 books and over 600 articles and left more than 150 unfinished manuscripts. He credited the Zettelkasten, but the site notes he also "worked all day, every day", so the method alone does not explain his output — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)
- Failure modes zettelkasten.de lists: ordinary note-taking "will create a bloated mess over time"; collecting links without intent is shallow work; copy-pasting skips the processing; note-writing can turn into procrastination and should stay tied to current projects; software lock-in ("boxing-in") — [zettelkasten.de Introduction](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)

**Ahrens' note types (How to Take Smart Notes, 2017). Secondary sources only.**
- Fleeting notes are temporary captures that only hold a thought until you process it. If unprocessed they are thrown away, and Ahrens expects them to be turned into permanent notes or discarded "within a day or two" — [Zettelkasten Forum](https://forum.zettelkasten.de/discussion/comment/14175/); [ybrikman summary](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/08/10/how-to-take-smart-notes/)
- Literature notes record a source's central idea "in your own words, as concisely as you can", with bibliographic details. They are short, selective and well-referenced — [ybrikman summary](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/08/10/how-to-take-smart-notes/); [binnyva notes](https://notes.binnyva.com/how-to-take-smart-notes/)
- Permanent notes hold one idea each, are understandable read in isolation, are written in your own words, and are linked to older notes — [ybrikman summary](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/08/10/how-to-take-smart-notes/)
- Terminology conflict: one forum reading says Ahrens' top-level categories are fleeting, permanent and *project* notes, with literature notes and Zettels as subtypes of permanent notes. zettelkasten.de notes that the word "Zettel" never appears in Ahrens. Other guides treat literature notes as a separate third type — [Zettelkasten Forum](https://forum.zettelkasten.de/discussion/comment/14175/); [zettelkasten.de on Ahrens' concepts](https://zettelkasten.de/posts/concepts-sohnke-ahrens-explained/)

**Evergreen notes (Andy Matuschak). Only principle titles were retrievable.**
- Evergreen notes are "written and organized to evolve, contribute, and accumulate over time, across projects." The aim is insight: "Better note-taking" misses the point; what matters is "better thinking" — [Evergreen notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes)
- Principle titles: "Evergreen notes should be atomic"; "...should be concept-oriented"; "...should be densely linked"; "Prefer associative ontologies to hierarchical taxonomies"; "Write notes for yourself by default, disregarding audience" — [Evergreen notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes)
- Diagnosis: "Most people take only transient notes." Related practices: "A reading inbox to capture possibly-useful references", "A writing inbox for transient and incomplete notes", "Write about what you read to internalize texts deeply", "Taxonomy of note types" — [Evergreen notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes)

**Progressive Summarization (Tiago Forte)**
- Layers: L0 is the original source. L1 captures anything insightful, by pasting, Kindle highlight export, web highlighters or forwarded email. L2 bolds key phrases and sentences. L3 highlights the "best of the best". L4 is an executive summary in your own words, done for only a few notes. A final "remix" step, for a tiny minority of sources, turns them into your own blog post, slide or video — [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)
- "Opportunistic compression": summarize a little at a time, during other work, and only as much as a note deserves. Layers 2–3 get added when you revisit a note anyway — [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)
- Compression makes notes discoverable and context makes them understandable. Over-compressing strips meaning; too much context buries the point. Keeping the earlier layers lets you cut without losing anything — [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)
- PARA (Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives) sorts notes by actionability and decides when they resurface. Progressive Summarization decides how each note is built — [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)

**Maps of Content / ACE (Nick Milo, LYT). Glossary and third-party summaries; the primary ACE page returned 404.**
- "The Mental Squeeze Point is when your unsorted knowledge becomes so messy it overwhelms and discourages you." The fix is to gather links to the related notes into a Map of Content (MOC) — [LYT Glossary](https://blog.linkingyourthinking.com/notes/lyt-glossary)
- A third-party rule of thumb is to create a MOC when a topic reaches about 5+ notes and starts to feel messy. This is a summary, not a Milo quote — [Obsidian Rocks](https://obsidian.rocks/maps-of-content-effortless-organization-for-notes/)
- ACE: Atlas holds timeless knowledge (links to content), Calendar is time-based (daily notes), Efforts are links to work done toward something — [DeveloPassion notes on ACE](https://notes.dsebastien.net/30+Areas/32+Literature+notes/32.02+Content/ACE+Framework)

**Collector's fallacy**
- Christian Tietze named the "Collector's Fallacy": gathering material feels productive, but knowing *about* something is not knowing it. Information becomes knowledge only once you merge it into your own thinking — [zettelkasten.de: Collector's Fallacy](https://www.zettelkasten.de/posts/collectors-fallacy)
- Practitioner account: after a decade of sorting, tagging and highlighting in Evernote, "you collected something doesn't mean you learned it or can explain it" — [Stefan Imhoff](https://stefanimhoff.de/zettelkasten-note-taking-devonthink/)
- Counterpoint: the real cost is opportunity cost. Time spent heavily processing one source could go to reading the next one — [Zettelkasten Forum thread](https://forum.zettelkasten.de/discussion/comment/9068)

### Inferences
- **Note types the app should support (minimum set):** `Inbox/Fleeting` (quick capture, voice, share-sheet) → `Source` (literature note tied to a Resource) → `Idea/Evergreen` (atomic, own words, claim-style title) → `Map` (MOC/structure note) → `Project/Effort note` (tied to a goal, learning area or content piece) → `Daily/Journal` (Calendar in ACE). This combines Ahrens, Matuschak and ACE without creating competing taxonomies.
- **Core Note fields:** `id` (immutable, so titles can change), `title` (one-sentence claim, e.g. "Spacing gaps should be ~10–20% of the retention interval"), `type`, `body`, `source_id` + locator (page/timestamp), `status` (inbox → processed → evergreen → archived), `summary_layer` (0–4, Forte), `links[]` each with a required `link_context` sentence (the "why"), backlinks (computed), `maps[]`, `efforts[]` (projects / learning areas / content pieces using it), `last_reviewed_at`, `review_due` (optional resurfacing).
- **Linking UX:** make the link reason a first-class field (zettelkasten.de's "the explicit statement of the why is created knowledge"). Prefer links and maps over folders, with at most a few top-level spaces (Atlas / Calendar / Efforts, or PARA). Keep tags for workflow state (e.g. `#to-process`), not topics.
- **MOC trigger:** when ≥5 notes share a link neighbourhood or tag and no Map covers them, suggest "Create a Map". AI can draft the map; the user edits it.
- **Anti-collector guardrails:** (1) a capture-to-processed ratio on the dashboard; (2) inbox items older than about 48 hours prompt "process, convert to task, or discard" (Ahrens' fleeting rule); (3) capture asks for a one-line "why am I saving this?"; (4) a reading-inbox cap or weekly triage; (5) every evergreen note should link to at least one existing note, and an orphan count is shown.
- **Anti-over-organizing:** no mandatory folder trees or tag taxonomies at setup. Structure appears only when the squeeze point is reached.
- **2024–2026 AI angle:** let AI do the low-value steps (transcribing voice notes, extracting highlights, proposing links, drafting L4 summaries and MOCs). Keep the user writing the atomic note in their own words, because own-words processing is where the understanding benefit is claimed to come from (zettelkasten.de, and the low utility of passive summarizing/highlighting in Dunlosky 2013, see the evidence section).

### Gaps
- No controlled studies found showing Zettelkasten, evergreen notes, PARA or MOCs improve long-term outcomes. Support is practitioner testimony.
- Matuschak's full note text (beyond titles) and the primary LYT ACE pages could not be retrieved, so the definitions above come from titles and glossary/third-party summaries.
- Forte's CODE (Capture–Organize–Distill–Express) is not described in the fetched article. Not separately sourced here.
- Didn't research laptop vs longhand note-taking studies (e.g., Mueller & Oppenheimer and the replications).

## How should a learning area be modeled (goal, resources, notes, practice sessions, progress) and connected to goals and content?

### Takeaway
Model a learning area as a project with a concrete performance goal. It needs an up-front "map" of what to learn and how (metalearning), a prioritized resource queue, practice sessions that favor direct practice, drills and self-testing over rereading, a gap list ("things I don't know"), retrieval items on a spaced schedule, and outputs (projects and public explanations) that feed the content pipeline.

### Cited Findings
- Ultralearning is "a strategy for aggressive, self-directed learning", meaning limited time and effort go to what works even when it is harder. Examples include Tristan de Montebello, who went from near-zero to a World Championship of Public Speaking finalist in seven months — [Scott H. Young: Ultralearning](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/ultralearning/)
- The nine principles (names consistent across summaries; wording varies): **Metalearning** (draw a map first, researching Why/What/How), **Focus**, **Directness** (learn by doing the real thing; project-based or immersive), **Drill** (attack your weakest point by isolating sub-skills), **Retrieval** (test to learn), **Feedback** (specific, corrective), **Retention** (don't fill a leaky bucket), **Intuition** (dig deep before building up), **Experimentation** — [Sloww summary](https://www.sloww.co/ultralearning-book/); [Dan Silvestre summary](https://dansilvestre.com/summaries/ultralearning/); [Medium summary](https://medium.com/@ahmadabrarsaleem/ultralearning-scott-youngs-9-principles-to-speed-learning-anything-by-ahmad-abrar-saleem-ab3f5e6b5f85)
- Feynman technique (Farnam Street version): explain the concept as if to a 6th grader, find gaps and go back to the source, organize and simplify, then optionally transmit it. FS says it adapted these steps from Feynman's method — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/feynman-learning-technique/)
- Origin evidence: per Gleick's biography (as quoted), Feynman titled a notebook "NOTEBOOK OF THINGS I DON'T KNOW ABOUT" and spent weeks taking each branch of physics apart, looking for raw edges. The test for a gap is where your explanation stalls or you reach for a term you can't define — [dsebastien: The Feynman Technique](https://dsebastien.net/blog/2022-08-03-the-feynman-technique)
- No controlled studies of the Feynman technique turned up. Support is anecdotal. However, the related techniques self-explanation and elaborative interrogation are rated "moderate utility" by Dunlosky et al. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013, PSPI](https://iverson.cm.utexas.edu/courses/310M/Handouts/Dunlosky%20et%20al.%20-%202013%20-%20Improving%20Students%E2%80%99%20Learning%20With%20Effective%20Learni.pdf)
- Matuschak recommends a "reading inbox to capture possibly-useful references" and a "writing inbox for transient and incomplete notes", i.e. a resource queue kept separate from the thinking notes — [Evergreen notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes)
- PARA separates Projects (active, with outcomes) from Areas (ongoing responsibilities) and Resources (topics of interest), sorted by actionability — [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)
- LYT's "Efforts" holds work in progress toward something, separate from timeless Atlas notes — [DeveloPassion notes on ACE](https://notes.dsebastien.net/30+Areas/32+Literature+notes/32.02+Content/ACE+Framework)
- Interleaved practice is rated "moderate utility". Practice testing and distributed practice are rated "high utility" — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 (American Educator version)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1021069.pdf)

### Inferences
- **LearningArea entity (fields):**
  - `name`, `type` (skill / course / book / domain), `status` (active / simmering / paused / done; similar to LYT effort intensity, but naming unverified)
  - `goal_statement` + `target_performance` (directness: "Record a 60-sec explainer with no notes", "Ship a feature in Rust") + `target_date`
  - `why` / `what` / `how` (metalearning map). `what` splits into **concepts / facts / procedures** (the common Ultralearning breakdown; not verified from the primary text, see Gaps)
  - `linked_goal_id` (company or life goal), `linked_content_pillar_ids` (which pillar this feeds)
  - `weekly_time_budget` (minutes), `time_logged` (rollup)
  - `progress` = completed skill-tree nodes / total, plus last benchmark score
- **SkillNode (skill tree / curriculum):** `title`, `parent_id`, `kind` (concept/fact/procedure), `status` (not started / learning / drilling / solid), `confidence` 1–5, `linked_notes[]`, `linked_cards[]`, `resources[]`, `weakest_point` flag (drill target).
- **Resource (read/watch-later queue):** `url/title/author`, `format` (course, book, video, podcast, article, paper, thread), `learning_area_id`, `skill_nodes[]`, `priority`, `est_minutes`, `status` (queued → active → done / abandoned), `progress %`, `why_saved` (required), `source_note_id` (literature note produced). Queue rules: a WIP limit on active resources (e.g. 1–2 per area); stale items auto-decay to "someday".
- **PracticeSession:** `date`, `duration`, `type` (study / drill / retrieval / direct project / feedback review / Feynman explanation), `skill_nodes[]`, `what I did`, `gaps found` (append to the area's **"Things I don't know" list**), `notes_created[]`, `cards_created[]`, `output_link` (project artifact or content draft), `energy/focus` 1–5.
- **RetrievalItem (flashcard / question):** `prompt`, `answer`, `source_note_id`, `skill_node_id`, FSRS-style scheduling fields (`due`, `stability`, `difficulty`, `last_rating`). AI can generate questions from notes (see the evidence section for caveats).
- **FeedbackLog:** `source` (mentor, audience comments, analytics, test score), `observation`, `action`. Audience comments on learn-in-public posts become feedback (see the learn-in-public section).
- **Connections:** Goal ↔ LearningArea ↔ SkillNodes ↔ Notes ↔ RetrievalItems. LearningArea ↔ ContentPillar so every session can emit a content seed ("TIL", "mistake I made", "explain X simply"). The weekly review shows minutes per area against budget, due cards, gaps added and closed, and content seeds generated.
- **Time-poor founder defaults:** 10–20 min daily retrieval queue + 2–3 direct-practice blocks a week + one "explain it" output a week that doubles as content.

### Gaps
- Scott Young's primary text for the nine principles, the exact metalearning map breakdown (concepts/facts/procedures) and any recommended share of time for metalearning research could not be verified (the primary page only covered the definition and examples).
- No sources found on established data models for "skill trees" in personal learning apps. The fields above are design inferences.
- No evidence base found specifically for read-later pipelines or resource-queue WIP limits.

## What are the stages and fields of a practical content pipeline (idea → research → script → record → edit → publish → repurpose → measure)? What metadata matters (platform, pillar, hook, format, status, performance)?

### Takeaway
Established creator systems agree on a few points. Keep a large always-on idea bank. Test ideas cheaply in short form, then expand winners into one long "pillar" piece a week. Script the hook with extra care. Move each piece through a status Kanban, cut the pillar into many platform-native micro pieces, and recycle top performers months later. The metadata that matters is pillar, platform, format, hook (type and text), status, dates, parent/child repurposing links, and staged performance snapshots (CTR, retention/AVD, engagement, follows/subs), with a short "lesson learned".

### Inferences
- **Pipeline stages (status enum):** `Idea` → `Seed (tested)` → `Brief/Research` → `Scripting` → `Script ready` → `Ready to record` → `Recorded` → `Editing` → `Ready to publish` (checklist gate) → `Scheduled` → `Published` → `Repurposing` → `Measured` → `Evergreen/Recycle-due` (resurface at +6 and +12 months, per Welsh) or `Archived`. Short text posts skip record/edit. The Kanban should hide stages that don't apply to a given format.
- **ContentPiece fields:** `working_title`, `pillar_id`, `platforms[]` (YouTube, Shorts, TikTok, IG Reels/Carousel, LinkedIn, X, Threads, Newsletter, Podcast), `format` (long video, short video, carousel, text post, thread, newsletter essay, podcast), `big_idea/angle`, `audience/persona`, `hook_text` + `hook_type` + hook layers (`visual`, `spoken`, `on_screen_text`), `title_options[]`, `thumbnail_concepts[]`, `CTA`, `script` (rich doc using a template), `source_notes[]` (knowledge-base links), `inspiration_refs[]` (swipe entries), `learning_area_id` (if learn-in-public), `assets` (raw footage, B-roll, thumbnail, captions/transcript), `batch_id`, `effort_estimate`, `due_date`, `publish_at`, `published_urls{platform: url}`, `parent_id` and `children[]` (repurposing tree), `status`, `lesson_learned`.
- **PerformanceSnapshot (child of a published platform post):** `captured_at` checkpoint (24h / 7d / 30d), `impressions`, `CTR`, `views`, `avg_view_duration` or `avg % viewed`, `3-sec hold / hook retention` (shorts), `likes`, `comments`, `shares`, `saves`, `follows/subs gained`, `link clicks / newsletter signups`, plus a computed `vs_my_median` (outlier score) so pieces are compared to your own baseline, following YouTube's "typical" comparison logic.
- **Script templates:** (1) Short video: Hook (0–3s, three layers) → Context/stakes → 3 value beats → Payoff → CTA. (2) Long video (HIVES-like): Hook (first 10–30s) → Intro/promise → Value sections → Ending/CTA. (3) Text post: Hook line → Re-hook → Body (list/story) → Takeaway → CTA. (4) Newsletter: Big idea → Story → Framework → Actions.
- **Publish checklist (gate on `Ready to publish`):** title/hook final, thumbnail (long-form), captions/subtitles, description + links + CTA, platform-specific aspect ratio, tags/hashtags, scheduled time, cross-post plan (children created), pinned comment, "notify list/newsletter".
- **Batching:** a "Batch session" entity groups pieces by stage (e.g. "write 5 hooks", "record 4 shorts") with a timer. The founder default (inferred from Welsh's and Koe's reported cadences) is 1 pillar a week + 3–7 short derivatives, with 1–2 recording batches a week.
- **Content calendar:** month/week views by `publish_at`, colored by pillar, with filters by platform/format and a "gap" warning when a pillar or platform goes quiet for N days.
- **Repurposing pyramid automation:** on `Published` for a pillar, auto-create child stubs (e.g. 3 shorts, 1 carousel, 2 text posts, 1 thread, 1 newsletter section). AI pre-fills them from the transcript (clip timestamps, post drafts), and each child links back to the parent.
- **Analytics review loop:** a weekly 20-minute review lists the top/bottom 3 pieces against your median, asks "why?" for each and writes a `lesson_learned`. Winners get `Recycle-due` dates and their hooks are promoted into the personal hook library.

- Ali Abdaal's own pipeline stage names (versus third-party) and the full HIVES acronym are unverified.
- Platform-specific analytics definitions for TikTok, Instagram, LinkedIn and X were not researched; only YouTube official metrics are sourced.

## How should saved inspiration (videos, reels, posts, threads) be captured and tagged so it is reusable (e.g., hook type, format, why it worked)?

### Takeaway
A swipe file only pays off if each saved item is broken down when you save it. Record the hook (text, plus visual, spoken and on-screen-text layers), the hook category, format and structure, a performance signal, and a required one-line "why it worked / what to steal". Link it to a pillar or idea. Otherwise it becomes another instance of the collector's fallacy.

### Cited Findings
- Hook categories commonly used: curiosity (knowledge gap), controversy (bold claim), relatable (mirrors viewer's experience), value (specific promised result), visual pattern interrupt — [quso.ai: Video hook ideas](https://quso.ai/blog/video-hook-ideas)
- Alternative hook taxonomy: question, result, mistake, myth, list, story, challenge — [Kudozz: 50 short-form hooks](https://www.kudozz.in/blog/short-form-video-hooks)
- Hook anatomy in three layers: Visual (what viewers see in the first frame), Spoken (first sentence), Text (on-screen words for silent viewers), each logged separately. This comes from vendor hook guides surfaced in search (exact page attribution among them not verified) — [Chaplin AI hooks guide](https://chaplinai.pro/en/foundation/hooks); [go-viral hook formulas](https://www.go-viral.app/blog/best-hooks-for-short-form-video/)
- An analysis of about 500 viral short videos classified hooks by verbal opening, visual opening, cognitive load and emotional axis (vendor analysis) — [Fluxnote: Short-form hook library](https://fluxnote.io/blog/short-form-hook-library-500-viral-videos)
- Testing hooks: post variants and compare 3-second retention or hold rate by platform; keep the spoken line fixed and vary the text overlay — [Kudozz](https://www.kudozz.in/blog/short-form-video-hooks); [CreatorsJet](https://www.creatorsjet.com/blog/7-viral-hook-frameworks-for-short-form-video-creators)
- Dan Koe reportedly keeps a swipe file of standout posts as an ideation input — [Applied AI Hub](https://appliedaihub.org/blog/how-dan-koe-uses-ai/)
- Ali Abdaal frames other people's content as a deliberate inspiration source ("Birdsong Technique") — [Ali Abdaal: Ultimate Guide to YouTube](https://aliabdaal.com/youtube/the-ultimate-guide-to-youtube/)
- Collector's fallacy applies: saving isn't learning — [zettelkasten.de](https://www.zettelkasten.de/posts/collectors-fallacy)
- Caveat: many hook "stats" (e.g. "65% leave within 3 seconds") come from marketing copy without clear methodology — [quso.ai](https://quso.ai/blog/video-hook-ideas) (noted across vendor results)

### Inferences
- **SwipeEntry fields:**
  - Capture: `url`, `platform`, `creator`, `captured_at`, `media_snapshot` (thumbnail/screenshot; downloading only where permitted), `transcript` (AI auto-transcribed), `first_3s_transcript`.
  - Hook: `hook_text`, `hook_category` (curiosity / contrarian / relatable / result-promise / question / mistake / myth / list / story / challenge / visual interrupt), `hook_layers {visual, spoken, on_screen_text}`, `emotion` (fear, aspiration, humor, outrage, awe, relief).
  - Body: `format` (talking head, green screen, voiceover B-roll, carousel, text post, thread, tutorial/screen record, skit, interview clip), `structure` (list, story, problem→solution, before/after, myth-bust, step-by-step, case study), `length_sec`, `pacing/edit notes`, `CTA type`.
  - Signal: `views/likes at capture`, `creator_follower_count` or creator median views → `outlier_ratio` (views ÷ creator median; more meaningful than raw views).
  - Reuse: `why_it_worked` (required, 1 sentence), `steal_this` (the transferable element), `pillar_ids[]`, `linked_idea_ids[]`, `used_in[]` (content pieces it inspired), `rating`.
- **Capture flows:** share-sheet / browser extension / paste URL → AI transcribes and pre-fills hook text, category, format and structure → user confirms and writes "why it worked" (one tap to accept AI's suggestion, but editable) → lands in a triage queue if skipped.
- **Hook library:** derived from SwipeEntries plus your own published hooks. Each hook becomes a template with blanks (e.g. "I spent [time] doing [X] so you don't have to") linked to its examples and, for your own hooks, your measured 3-sec hold/CTR. The editor offers "insert hook from library" filtered by pillar, format and category.
- **Anti-hoarding:** a weekly "swipe → idea" conversion prompt and a stat for swipes saved vs swipes used in pieces.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or platform-official taxonomy of hook types was found; taxonomies come from vendor/creator blogs.
- Legal/ToS constraints on downloading and storing other creators' videos per platform were not researched (relevant to the `media_snapshot` feature).

## How do the best systems turn learning notes into content ("learn in public")?

### Takeaway
The working pattern is to publish "learning exhaust" while you learn. Write a short explanation of what you just learned (Feynman-style), test it as a short post, expand the ones that resonate into a pillar piece, then repurpose it. Audience questions and analytics feed back as gaps to learn next. Forte's "remix" layer, Koe's seed→newsletter→video ladder and GaryVee's pyramid are all versions of this path.

### Cited Findings
- swyx: publish what you learn as you learn it, since you will never be "done". Most people "learn in private" and lurk. Produce "learning exhaust": blogs, tutorials, meetup talks, answering questions on Stack Overflow/Reddit, videos/streams, a newsletter. Avoid closed platforms (Slack/Discord) because they aren't public — [swyx: Learn In Public](https://swyx.io/learn-in-public)
- swyx's follow-up "Learning Gears" describes modes: Explorer (mostly private notes), Connector (regular material for others), Miner (deep focus on something few others study) — [swyx: Learning Gears](https://swyx.io/learning-gears)
- swyx on mindset: separate your ego from your work. You can learn a lot online "for the low price of your ego" — [Career Chats: Learn in Public](https://careerchats.transistor.fm/episodes/learn-in-public)
- A common summary of learn in public: make the resource you wish you had found while learning — [dsebastien: Learn in Public concept](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/learn-in-public/)
- Forte: for a tiny minority of powerful sources, the final step is to "remix" them with your own perspective into a blog post, sketch, slide or video — [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)
- Feynman step 4 ("transmit") is an optional teaching/sharing step after simplifying — [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/feynman-learning-technique/)
- Koe: short posts as tests → resonant "seeds" → long-form newsletter → YouTube script and other derivatives — [Startup Anatomy](https://startupanatomy.substack.com/p/how-dan-koe-built-a-3m-one-person); [Irene Pardo, Medium](https://medium.com/@irenepardo/dan-koes-blueprint-for-infinite-content-from-a-single-idea-4931e25aee59)
- GaryVee's loop includes "listen" and "apply insights" after distribution — [Biteable](https://biteable.com/blog/gary-vees-content-framework-one-video-30-content-pieces/)
- Matuschak's "Write notes for yourself by default, disregarding audience" is a counterweight: evergreen notes are private thinking first, and public output comes from them — [Evergreen notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes)

### Inferences
- **The connecting flow in the app ("Learn → Note → Post → Pillar → Learn"):**
  1. A PracticeSession or Resource completion asks: "What's one thing you can now explain?" → creates an evergreen Note (own words).
  2. A "Make it public" action on any Note runs a Feynman draft (AI simplifies to a 6th-grade explanation and flags undefined jargon as gaps), then generates 3 short-post variants (TIL / mistake / myth-bust) using the hook library. The result is a ContentPiece in `Seed` with `source_notes[]` and `learning_area_id` set.
  3. The seed is published as a short text post (cheapest test). After 24–72h its PerformanceSnapshot is compared to your median.
  4. If it's an outlier, the app suggests "Promote to pillar": a newsletter/long video brief pre-filled with linked notes, MOC context and audience comments.
  5. The pillar is published and the repurposing pyramid auto-stubs its children.
  6. Audience questions and comments become feedback entries and "things I don't know" gaps in the LearningArea, which closes the loop.
- **Pillar ↔ Learning area mapping:** each content pillar has 1–3 learning areas feeding it (e.g. pillar "Building a startup" ← areas "Fundraising", "Hiring"; pillar "AI for founders" ← "LLM engineering"), so learning time also produces content.
- **Privacy default:** notes are private by default (Matuschak). Publishing is an explicit transform, not a toggle on the note.
- **Time-poor framing:** the "minimum viable learn-in-public" is one short TIL post per learning session plus one weekly pillar built from the best-performing TIL.

### Gaps
- No empirical studies found measuring learning gains from learning in public (the protégé/teaching effect literature was not researched).
- No primary documentation of how Ali Abdaal or Justin Welsh link specific learning notes to content was retrieved.

## What is the evidence for spaced repetition, retrieval practice and note-taking methods?

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence in this set is for practice testing (retrieval) and distributed practice (spacing). Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate both "high utility". Self-explanation, elaborative interrogation and interleaving are "moderate". Highlighting, summarization and rereading, the habits most PKM tools encourage, are "low". Optimal spacing grows with how long you need to remember. FSRS-type schedulers reach the same retention with fewer reviews than SM-2. No controlled evidence was found for specific PKM methods (Zettelkasten, PARA, MOCs).

### Cited Findings
**Dunlosky et al. (2013), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 4–58**
- 10 techniques rated: **High utility:** practice testing, distributed practice. **Moderate:** elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. **Low:** summarization, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning, rereading — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 (PDF)](https://iverson.cm.utexas.edu/courses/310M/Handouts/Dunlosky%20et%20al.%20-%202013%20-%20Improving%20Students%E2%80%99%20Learning%20With%20Effective%20Learni.pdf); [American Educator summary (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1021069.pdf)
- High-utility techniques help learners of different ages and abilities across many criterion tasks. Most students report rereading and highlighting, but these do not consistently improve performance. In controlled studies, highlighting failed to help U.S. Air Force trainees, children and remedial students — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 (PDF)](https://www.whz.de/fileadmin/lehre/hochschuldidaktik/docs/dunloskiimprovingstudentlearning.pdf); [Scientific American Mind summary](https://wcer.wisc.edu/docs/resources/cesa2017/Dunlosky_SciAmMind.pdf)

**Retrieval practice**
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), "Test-Enhanced Learning", *Psychological Science* 17(3), 249–255: a secondary summary reports about 61% retention of a passage after one week for repeated recall versus about 40% for repeated rereading, and meta-analytic advantages around g = 0.50–0.61 (secondary figures; verify against the primary) — [ResearchGate: Test-Enhanced Learning](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7270829_Test-Enhanced_Learning_Taking_Memory_Tests_Improves_Long-Term_Retention); [Yu-kai Chou summary](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/retrieval-practice-testing-effect-roediger-karpicke-learning/)
- Karpicke & Roediger (2007), *Journal of Memory and Language*: "Repeated retrieval during learning is the key to long-term retention" (title states the finding) — [Purdue Learning Lab PDF](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2007/2007_Karpicke_Roediger_JML.pdf)

**Spacing**
- Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis: one secondary source describes it as a quantitative review of 254 studies with over 14,000 observations (figure unverified against the abstract). Expanding intervals beat uniform ones, but large standard errors mean high between-study variability, so the expanding-vs-uniform result is hard to interpret — [lscp.net: Meta-analytic review of spacing](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR20.pdf); [ERIC: Optimizing Distributed Practice](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505659.pdf)
- Cepeda et al. (2008) "temporal ridgeline": gaps of 0–105 days and test delays of 7–350 days. The optimal gap scales with the retention interval: 7-day test → about 1-day gap; 35-day → about 11 days; 70-day → about 21 days. Roughly, the optimal gap ≈ 10–20% of the test delay. The study covered only two learning sessions — [ERIC: Using Spacing to Enhance Diverse Forms of Learning](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED536925.pdf); [ERIC: Optimizing Distributed Practice](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505659.pdf) (figures obtained via search summaries of these reviews)

**Spaced-repetition software (FSRS vs SM-2)**
- Anki's official FAQ: with FSRS, users need fewer reviews than with Anki's default (SM-2-based) algorithm to reach the same retention — [Anki FAQ: What spaced repetition algorithm](https://docs.ankiweb.net/faqs/what-spaced-repetition-algorithm)
- FSRS default parameters were fitted on about 727 million reviews from about 10k users; benchmarks show fewer reviews needed for the same retention. The optimizer is open source and anyone can benchmark their own history — [Wikipedia: Anki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anki)
- A vendor estimate puts savings at about 20–30% fewer reviews (vendor claim, not independent) — [RemNote: FSRS](https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/9124137-the-fsrs-spaced-repetition-algorithm)
- Caveat: savings depend on whether actual retention matches the algorithm's target — [Anki Forums](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/new-progress-in-implement-the-custom-algorithm/22961/38)

**AI-generated retrieval practice (2023–2025 research exists; results not verified here)**
- Studies exist on LLM-generated retrieval-practice questions in data science courses and on implementing learning principles with a personal AI tutor. Their findings were not read in these notes — [arXiv 2507.05629](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.05629); [arXiv 2309.13060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.13060)

**Note-taking methods**
- No controlled evidence was found for Zettelkasten/PARA/MOCs. The closest evidence is indirect: summarization and highlighting rate low utility as learning techniques (Dunlosky), while self-explanation and elaborative interrogation rate moderate — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 (PDF)](https://iverson.cm.utexas.edu/courses/310M/Handouts/Dunlosky%20et%20al.%20-%202013%20-%20Improving%20Students%E2%80%99%20Learning%20With%20Effective%20Learni.pdf)
- Feynman technique: no controlled studies found; anecdotal support only — [dsebastien: The Feynman Technique](https://dsebastien.net/blog/2022-08-03-the-feynman-technique)

### Inferences
- **Product implications:**
  - Make **retrieval the default way to revisit notes**: a "Review" mode that hides a note's body and asks the user to recall it (from the claim-style title) before revealing it. This turns PKM browsing into practice testing.
  - **Spaced queue via FSRS**: use an open-source FSRS implementation for RetrievalItems, with a user-set target retention (e.g. 85–90%) and a daily cap (e.g. 10–15 min) for a time-poor founder.
  - **Don't reward highlighting**: highlights and L1–L3 compression help discoverability (Forte), but the app shouldn't present them as learning. Credit "learning" for retrieval, self-explanation (own-words notes, Feynman drafts) and direct practice.
  - **Spacing for non-flashcard learning**: schedule resurfacing of evergreen notes and skill nodes with gaps of about 10–20% of the horizon the user cares about (e.g. a pitch in 30 days → revisit every ~3–6 days), following Cepeda 2008.
  - **Interleaving**: the daily review queue mixes cards across learning areas instead of grouping by deck (moderate-utility evidence).
  - **AI question generation**: auto-generate 2–3 recall questions per new evergreen note for user approval. Quality checks are needed because the evidence for LLM-generated questions wasn't verified here.

### Gaps
- Primary-source verification of the Roediger & Karpicke (2006) percentages and the Cepeda (2006) sample size was not done. Figures come from secondary summaries, and the reported meta-analysis size may differ from the primary abstract.
- No independent peer-reviewed comparison of FSRS vs SM-2 was found beyond Anki's documentation and community benchmarks.
- No controlled studies of Zettelkasten/evergreen/PKM outcomes, the Feynman technique, or learning in public were found.
- Didn't research the laptop vs longhand note-taking literature or generative note-taking (e.g., concept mapping) studies.
