# Give Each Framework Exactly One Job

Don't pick one productivity system. The best design for a founder life OS stacks several proven frameworks and gives each one a single job. **Life Areas** (Wheel of Life, GTD Areas of Focus and PARA Areas, which are the same idea) form the spine. A vision and up to eight annual goals narrow down to **no more than three active 12-week goals**. Each one has a key result (the outcome you want) and weekly tactics (the actions you control). GTD runs capture, projects, next actions and "waiting for". PARA and atomic notes hold knowledge. A Weekly Big 3 and a Daily Big 3, placed into maker and manager time blocks, run execution. One merged weekly review closes the loop. A founder who also delivers, sells and markets needs four business engines on top of this spine. The first is a sales pipeline where **every open deal must have a dated next step**. The second is a commitments ledger of what you owe and what others owe you. The third is a content pipeline that turns sales calls, saved videos and learning into posts. The fourth is a delegation module (time audit, Buyback Rate, written processes) that later becomes the company layer. The research behind the core parts is solid. Specific, hard goals beat "do your best" (effect sizes d = .42 to .80) ([Locke & Latham 2002](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20%282002%29.pdf)). If-then plans raise goal attainment (d = .65) ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)). Recorded progress tracking helps (d = .40) ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)). The famous numbers (85% execution, "Big 3", "three goals") are rules of thumb from practitioners, not research findings. The biggest risk is not picking the wrong framework. It is quitting the system: David Allen calls falling off "almost universal" ([David Allen](https://davidallen.substack.com/p/partial-or-complete-gtd)). So the app must defend against review lapse, maintenance work, over-planning and guilt from the first release. Build it in five phases: the daily loop, then goals and habits, then the founder business engines, then knowledge and learning, and last the shared company layer.

## Frameworks fit together once each one owns a single layer

Every framework in this field mixes three parts: an **outcome layer** (vision, goals, OKRs, 12-week goals), an **inventory layer** (projects, next actions, logs, backlogs), and a **daily rule for choosing what to do** (contexts, Big 3, Ivy Lee's six, 1-3-5, "eat the frog", time blocks, work-in-progress limits). GTD and Bullet Journal start from the inventory and work upward. Full Focus, the 12 Week Year and OKRs start from goals and work downward. Time blocking, Ivy Lee, 1-3-5 and Kanban are only execution rules. Trouble starts when two frameworks try to own the same layer. So the design rule is simple: one owner per layer.

GTD already gives the skeleton. Its Horizons of Focus go from purpose and vision (reviewed "whenever additional clarity… is needed"), to 12–24 month goals (reviewed "annually; quarterly reviews and recalibrations"), to areas of focus ("monthly personal check-in's"), to projects (the Weekly Review), to next actions (checked "multiple times daily"). The same document states that "priorities are determined from the top down" ([GTD Horizons of Focus](https://gettingthingsdone.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/2016-Levels-of-Your-Work.pdf)). But GTD describes its upper levels thinly, so the other frameworks fill gaps instead of fighting it. Full Focus adds the clearest number cascade: **eight annual goals, no more than three per quarter** (more "risks burnout"), then a Weekly Big 3 and a Daily Big 3 ([Full Focus](https://fullfocus.co/system/)). The 12 Week Year adds the best feedback loop: one to three goals per 12 weeks, weekly tactics, and a weekly **execution score = tactics done ÷ tactics planned**, with 85% as the bar ([12 Week Year book notes](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18961505-the-12-week-year/153805008-javy?page=2)). OKRs add outcome grading from 0 to 1.0, where 0.6–0.7 counts as success ([OKR primer](https://speakerdeck.com/escapist/okrs-a-primer)). The 12 Week Year scores *actions* and OKRs score *results*, so one goal can carry both scores without conflict.

| Layer | Owner framework(s) | What it holds | Review rhythm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose and values | GTD Horizon 5; Newport's values document | Why you work, your standards | Yearly, or when you feel lost |
| Vision (3–5 years) | GTD Horizon 4; EOS-style one-page vision | Picture of success, per area if wanted | Yearly |
| **Life Areas (the spine)** | Wheel of Life; GTD Horizon 2; PARA Areas | 6–10 areas, each with a "standard" and a 1–10 rating | Monthly |
| Annual goals | Full Focus (up to 8); GTD Horizon 3 | Specific goal, ranked reasons, first actions | Yearly, checked quarterly |
| 12-week goals | 12 Week Year; Full Focus Quarterly Big 3; OKRs; EOS Rocks | Max 3 active; key result (lag) plus weekly tactics (lead) | Scored weekly, graded at quarter end |
| Projects | GTD Horizon 1 = PARA Projects; Personal Kanban | Multi-step outcomes; one area, optional goal; WIP cap | Weekly |
| Actions and commitments | GTD next actions and Waiting For; EOS To-Dos | Tasks with "when", deadline, estimate, energy; promises | Daily |
| Week plan | Full Focus Weekly Big 3; Ideal Week; theme days | Three weekly outcomes placed on a template week | Weekly |
| Day plan | Daily Big 3, Ivy Lee, 1-3-5 or frog; Newport time blocks | A small "daily shape" placed into blocks | Daily |
| Reference | PARA Resources and Archives; Building a Second Brain; Zettelkasten | Notes, sources, saved media | Inbox weekly, refine when used |

Practitioners already build combinations like this. One GTD forum user runs GTD with the 12 Week Year because "they complement each other perfectly". He maps 12-week tactics to GTD next actions and caps himself at 14 tactics a week ([GTD forum](https://forum.gettingthingsdone.com/posts/145854)). Thomas Frank's Ultimate Brain puts tasks, projects, notes and goals in one Notion workspace, with PARA structure and "GTD-style processing" ([Ultimate Brain](https://thomasjfrank.com/brain)).

Four conflicts need a clear product decision. **First**, GTD picks the next action in the moment by context, time, energy and then priority ([GTD: choosing what to do](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2023/01/choosing-what-to-do/)), while Full Focus and Newport commit to priorities and blocks in advance. GTD's own model already allows "pre-defined work" alongside work that shows up. The answer is to time-block the Big 3 and goal tactics, and keep a few context blocks (calls, errands, admin) where GTD-style lists rule. **Second**, Bullet Journal's monthly migration works because rewriting by hand is a filter: "If an entry isn't worth the effort to rewrite, then it's probably not that important" ([Bullet Journal](https://bulletjournal.com/blogs/faq/migration)). Silent digital rollover destroys that filter. The app should force a keep, schedule or drop decision on tasks that have waited for weeks. **Third**, goal counts differ: Full Focus allows eight a year but three a quarter, the 12 Week Year one to three, OKRs three to five objectives ([Shortform: Measure What Matters](https://shortform.com/summary/measure-what-matters-summary-john-doerr)), and EOS three to seven company Rocks. For one person, the default should be **three active quarterly goals at most**, with the annual list as a backlog. **Fourth**, the Eisenhower matrix (urgent vs important) is a sorting lens for the inbox and the weekly review, not a separate system.

Habits and routines should not live in their own silo. Model them as one "recurring commitment" object with one of two parents. Under an **Area**, a habit is a maintenance standard ("lift weights three times a week" under Health), and misses lower that area's health. Under a **goal**, it is a 12 Week Year lead measure ("20 outreach messages a day" under a revenue goal), and it counts toward the weekly execution score. The 12 Week Year itself names workouts and sales calls as lead measures for weight and revenue ([12 Week Year book notes](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18961505-the-12-week-year/153805008-javy?page=2)). Also, most of a founder's tasks belong to an Area, not a goal: invoices, client support, tax, health. Forcing every task to roll up to a goal goes against GTD's Areas level and invites fake goals. A project with no goal is simply a maintenance project.

The founder-specific frameworks shape the week and the business modules rather than the hierarchy. Paul Graham's maker/manager split and Dan Sullivan's Free/Focus/Buffer days shape the Ideal Week. Jack Dorsey's theme days become a domain tag on every block. Dan Martell's *Buy Back Your Time* and EOS's Delegate and Elevate shape the delegation module. A light version of EOS (Rocks, Scorecard, To-Dos, Issues) shapes the weekly review and seeds the company layer. The next section covers each one.

### The research supports the core moves, not the famous numbers

Goal-setting theory is the strongest base. In meta-analyses, specific, difficult goals beat "do your best" with effect sizes from **d = .42 to .80**. Harder goals produced more effort and performance until ability or commitment ran out ([Locke & Latham 2002](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20%282002%29.pdf)). The same review gives four design rules. Goals plus feedback work better than goals alone. Commitment matters most when goals are hard. Adding near-term sub-goals to a distant goal raised self-efficacy and profits in a business game. And on complex, new tasks, specific *learning* goals ("find five strategies") beat outcome goals, which can cause "evaluative pressure and performance anxiety." That last point matters for founders, because much of their work is new. The app should offer goals like "run 10 pricing experiments" next to "reach $X monthly revenue."

Three other findings are strong enough to build into the product. If-then plans ("If it is 9:00 and I open my laptop, then I write 20 outreach messages") raised goal attainment by **d = .65 across 94 tests** ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)). Mental contrasting plus if-then plans (WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan) adds a smaller effect, **g = 0.336 across 15,907 people**. It worked better when delivered through interaction with a person (g = 0.465) than through documents (g = 0.277) ([Wang et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8149892/)). Prompting people to monitor progress raised goal attainment by **d = 0.40 across 138 trials**, with larger effects when progress was physically recorded or reported to others ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)).

The research also shows what not to believe. People usually hold 10–15 personal goals at once. Conflict between goals is linked to poorer performance and lower well-being, but the literature is "scattered" ([Gorges & Grund 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02011/full)). So a cap of three active goals fits the research but does not come from it. The 12 Week Year's 85% bar is the authors' own observation from clients. Newport's claim that a time-blocked 40-hour week equals 60+ unstructured hours is his estimate, with no data ([Cal Newport](https://calnewport.com/deep-habits-the-importance-of-planning-every-minute-of-your-work-day/)). The often-quoted "1953 Yale goals study" never happened ([Tability](https://www.tability.io/odt/articles/goal-setting-theory)). No controlled study tests GTD, PARA, Full Focus or the 12 Week Year as complete systems. The app should therefore enforce the mechanics with evidence behind them (specific goals, feedback, near-term sub-goals, if-then plans, visible tracking) and treat the numbers as defaults the user can edit.

## A near-solo founder's week runs on half-day blocks and two engines

### Protect maker mornings, group calls, and plan Free Days first

No source gives a tested time split for a founder with almost no team. What exists are strong patterns. Paul Graham's maker's schedule works in "units of half a day at least," because "a single meeting can blow a whole afternoon." His fixes are office hours and meetings at the end of the day ([Paul Graham](http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html)). Jack Dorsey, while running both Twitter and Square, gave each weekday one theme (Monday management, Tuesday product, Wednesday marketing and growth, and so on), so any interruption had an obvious home ([CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2011/11/13/technology/dorsey_techonomy/)). Dan Sullivan splits days into **Free Days** (no work at all, midnight to midnight), **Focus Days** for revenue work and **Buffer Days** for preparation and admin, and warns: "Just don't have an unplanned Free Day" ([Strategic Coach](https://charm.strategiccoach.com/resources/podcasts/inside-strategic-coach/135794272)). On building vs selling, *Traction*'s "50% rule" says to spend about half your effort on getting customers from day one ([Startup Grind](https://startupgrind.com/blog/the-50-rule)). Even big-company CEOs struggle to protect focus time. Porter and Nohria's study of 27 CEOs (as reported in secondary coverage) found about **62.5 working hours a week**, with only 28% of work time spent alone, mostly in blocks of an hour or less ([MGMA](https://www.mgma.com/articles/test-of-time-harvard-study-reveals-how-ceos-spend-their-days-on-and-off-work)).

Full theme days do not fit a founder who delivers client work, because client deadlines and buyers' calendars do not follow one theme per day. A hybrid works better: maker mornings for delivery and writing, manager afternoons for calls grouped into office hours, a daily sales block, one weekly content block, and a Friday review. As a starting point (this report's synthesis, not a research finding), plan about **40–50% of work time for delivery, 25–30% for sales, 10–15% for marketing and content, and about 10% for company building and admin**. Then re-weight each week by comparing pipeline coverage with delivery load. The app should mark each block as maker or manager, tag it with a domain, show target vs actual hours by domain, and warn when a new meeting would split a maker block.

| Day | Morning (maker) | Afternoon (manager) |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | 30–45 min weekly plan, then delivery block | Client calls in office hours |
| Tuesday | Delivery block | Outreach and discovery calls |
| Wednesday | Content hub writing (2–3 h) | Sales calls, then pipeline review (60–90 min) |
| Thursday | Delivery block | Client calls, then cut hub into short posts (60–90 min) |
| Friday | Buffer half-day: processes, recordings, finance | Weekly review (60–90 min), co-founder sync (30–60 min) |
| Every workday | 15-min start-up; 60–90 min outreach and follow-up; 15–30 min content engagement; 10-min shutdown | |
| Weekend | At least one protected Free Day; optional short Sunday reflection | |

### Sales: a short pipeline where no deal lacks a dated next step

Pete Kazanjy's *Founding Sales* gives the most usable mechanics. Stages need "crisp definitions," and "every stage needs an explicit next step... with a set date and time." Pipelines fall apart when deals have no next step. He runs a two-hour weekly pipeline review plus a "floating hour a day" for follow-up. He flags deals with **no activity for 7 days**, deals stuck **about two weeks** in one stage, and contracts out for **about 9 days** ([Founding Sales](https://holloway.com/g/founding-sales/sections/pipeline-management)). A lean founder version starts with five stages and adds a stage only when a real deal needs it ([Cut the SaaS](https://cut-the-saas.com/guides/how-to-build-a-sales-pipeline)). Jen Abel adds two habits. Book the next call before you hang up, because "email you later" often means no interest. And watch win rate more than raw conversion, because qualification is "the biggest opportunity for improvement" ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/master-founder-led-sales-jen-abel)). Alex Hormozi's "Rule of 100" (100 primary actions a day, such as outreach attempts or minutes of content) is the best-known quota. But he says it takes most people "about 4 hours a day ish" ([Modern Wisdom](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/modern-wisdom/830-alex-hormozi-24-controversial-truths-about-success-failure)), which is too much for someone who also delivers.

The design follows from this. Use the stages Target → Contacted → Conversation booked → Discovery done (pain, budget and decision-maker confirmed) → Proposal sent → Verbal yes → Won, Lost or Unqualified. The app should **refuse to save an open deal without a next step and a date**. It should raise Kazanjy's flags automatically and feed "next steps due today" into the daily sales block. The outreach quota should be adjustable (for example 20–30 touches a day during heavy delivery). It should also have an "open to goal" mode that ends the block once a set number of meetings is booked, which is Hormozi's own advanced version. A won deal should create a delivery project with its commitments. A lost or cold deal should become a nurture contact instead of disappearing.

### Content: one hub piece a week, cut into many short pieces

People who run one-person media businesses end up with the same hub-and-spoke pattern. Justin Welsh sells a system for one newsletter plus "6–12 pieces of high-performance social media content each week" ([Justin Welsh](https://learn.justinwelsh.me/content)). A case study reports that he runs his business on about 10 hours a week with one part-time assistant (his own figures) ([Built by Foundry](https://www.builtbyfoundry.io/blog/justin-welsh-solopreneur-12m-empire)). Dan Koe tests ideas as short posts, expands the winners into a weekly newsletter, then turns that into a video script and other formats ([Startup Anatomy](https://startupanatomy.substack.com/p/how-dan-koe-built-a-3m-one-person)). Reports of his writing time conflict, ranging from under an hour to about two hours a day ([Trends.vc](https://trends.vc/how-does-dan-koe-get-millions-of-views-writing-just-2-hours-a-day/)). Gary Vaynerchuk's "reverse pyramid" cuts one pillar piece into many short pieces for each platform, then listens and repeats ([GaryVee](https://garyvaynerchuk.com/THE-GARYVEE-CONTENT-STRATEGY-HOW-TO-GROW-AND-DISTRIBUTE-YOUR-BRANDS-SOCIAL-MEDIA-CONTENT/)). Welsh reportedly republishes his best posts 6 and 12 months later ([Tweet Hunter](https://tweethunter.io/blog/justin-welsh-content-repurposing-system)).

For a founder who is also delivering, a realistic budget is **4–6 hours a week**. That means one 2–3 hour maker block for the hub piece, one 60–90 minute block to cut it into 5–10 short posts with fixed templates, and 15–30 minutes a day for posting, comments and DMs, which also works as warm outreach. The key link is with sales. Every objection and question from a sales call should land in the content idea bank. Every lead should carry a source channel, so the founder can see whether content actually produces conversations.

### Commitments, relationships and decisions need a ledger

Broken promises to clients are expensive, so commitments need their own object. GTD's Waiting For list records what you need from others, from whom, and when you will check again, and the weekly review sweeps it ([Super Productivity](https://super-productivity.com/blog/gtd-waiting-for-list/)). EOS adds the 7-day To-Do: done or not done, never on the list longer than two weeks, with **about 90% cleared each week** ([EOS Level 10](https://www.eosworldwide.com/blog/level-10-meeting)). Combine these into one Commitment record with a direction (I owe / owed to me), the other person, the source (call, email, meeting), a promised date, a check-again date and a link to the project or deal. Report a weekly promise-keeping rate. For relationships, a cadence tag per person (weekly = 7 days, monthly = 30, quarterly = 90, twice a year = 182, yearly = 365) creates a "due to reconnect" list ([Intentional Relationship template](https://valentinperez.notion.site/Intentional-Relationship-Aid-Template-df40dfccf65b41b48dfce4391ef2817b)). The last-contact date can be filled in automatically from dated notes and meetings, as the Obsidian Personal CRM plugin does ([Obsidian Personal CRM](https://community.obsidian.md/plugins/personal-crm)). For big choices, Farnam Street's decision journal records the situation, the alternatives, the expected outcome with probabilities ("The degree of confidence matters, a lot") and your physical and mental state. It must be written *before* acting, so hindsight cannot rewrite your reasoning ([Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)). The app should schedule a review of each decision some weeks later.

### Delegation readiness is the bridge to the company layer

Dan Martell's *Buy Back Your Time* gives a clear sequence. The details here come from summaries and an interview, not the book itself. Log your work every 15 minutes for two weeks, and rate each task from $ to $$$$ and as energizing or draining ([Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/time-audit/)). Sort tasks into the DRIP quadrants: Delegation, Replacement, Investment, Production ([SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/buy-back-your-time/summary/)). His **Buyback Rate = annual income ÷ 2,000 hours ÷ 4**. At $100,000 that is $50 an hour and a Buyback Rate of $12.50. You should not do work you could pay someone that rate or less to do ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/living/how-much-should-pay-other-people-to-take-work-off-your/443766)). His Replacement Ladder hands off admin first, then delivery, marketing, sales and leadership. His Camcorder Method records you doing a task so the new person can write up the process ([Readingraphics](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-buy-back-your-time/)). EOS's Delegate and Elevate asks that **at least 80% of your activities** sit in the two top quadrants (things you love or like and do well) ([EOS](https://www.eosworldwide.com/delegate-and-elevate)). Its Process Documenter limits each core process to the 20% of steps that produce 80% of results, usually 7–15 major steps, with 5–12 core processes per company ([EOS Process Documenter](https://www.eosworldwide.com/three-step-process-documenter)).

In the app, a task marked "delegate" should prompt "record this next time you do it." The recording becomes a draft process document (SOP). Each ladder rung should show a **readiness score**: the share of that rung's recurring tasks that already have a written process. This tells the founder when a first assistant or contractor could take a rung over. For this founder, the first two core processes are client delivery (onboarding, kickoff, milestones, handover) and lead-to-close.

### Take EOS-lite now and leave the rest for later

EOS is built for companies of roughly 10–250 people with a leadership team. Full adoption takes about two years, and below about 10 people it can feel like overhead ([Strety](https://strety.com/blog/evaluating-eos-for-small-to-mid-size-companies/)). A near-solo founder should take only the light tools now. These are a one-page vision, three to five quarterly Rocks with milestones at day 30, 60 and 90 ([EOS Implementer](https://implementer.eosworldwide.com/implementer-post/the-basics-of-setting-eos-smart-rocks/)), a Scorecard of 5–10 weekly numbers (EOS uses "five to fifteen"), 7-day To-Dos, and an Issues list worked with IDS: Identify, Discuss, Solve ([EOS Level 10](https://www.eosworldwide.com/blog/level-10-meeting)). EOS says the chance of finishing at least 80% of Rocks "decrease[s] significantly" above seven ([EOS Rocks](https://www.eosworldwide.com/blog/creating-the-best-rocks)). With a co-founder, a 30–60 minute weekly sync on the Level 10 skeleton (scorecard, Rocks, headlines, To-Dos, IDS) matches the length of EOS department meetings. The Accountability Chart should wait, except for naming the two co-founders' seats. So should the People Analyzer, the full 90-minute Level 10, Scaling Up's daily huddles ([Align](https://aligntoday.com/the-meeting-rhythm-to-scale-up-efficiently/)) and cascading priorities. They need people to hold accountable. But build Rocks, metrics, To-Dos, Issues and processes with an **owner field from day one**, even if the owner is always "me." Then the company layer only adds people. It does not require a rebuild.

## About twenty linked objects carry every part of life and work

The tools that last have settled on a small set of typed objects joined by links, with the **day as the time hub**. Tana turns any note into a typed object with fields through "supertags" ([Tana](https://tana.inc/docs/supertags)). Capacities asks you to pick an object type first ("We work with objects and object types rather than files and folders") and treats the calendar as "the time-based hub" ([Capacities](https://docs.capacities.io/tutorials/getting-started)). Notion life-OS templates teach a clear lesson: fix the spine in code. Thomas Frank's popular Ultimate Brain stores Areas inside a Tags database and links them to tasks only indirectly ([Ultimate Brain FAQ](https://thomasjfrank.com/docs/ultimate-brain/ultimate-brain-faqs/)). An ideal system makes Area a first-class object, ships the spine pre-built, and allows only light custom fields.

| Object | Key fields | Main links |
|---|---|---|
| **Area** | Name, standard ("what maintained means"), 1–10 rating, owner | Goals, projects, habits, notes |
| Purpose / Vision | Free text, optional per area | Areas |
| **Goal** (annual or 12-week) | Statement with a number or date, type (outcome or learning), ranked reasons, key results (lag), tactics (lead), status | One area; projects; habits; learning areas |
| **Project** | Outcome, status (backlog, active, waiting, done), WIP cap | One area; zero or one goal; tasks; notes; deal or client |
| **Task** | "When" date, deadline, estimate, energy (high/medium/low), context, tactic flag | One project or one area |
| **Commitment** | Direction (I owe / owed to me), person, source, promised date, check-again date | Person, deal, project |
| **Habit / Routine** | If-then plan, anchor, minimum/standard/stretch versions, target with reserve skips | One area (standard) or one goal (tactic) |
| **Day** and **Time block** | Day type (Focus, Buffer, Free); block type (maker, manager); domain tag | Tasks done, check-in, journal, captures |
| **Review** | Type (daily to annual), scores, answers | Goals, areas, week, quarter |
| **Person / Company** | Relationship tier and cadence, last contact | Deals, commitments, meetings, notes |
| **Deal** | Stage, next step + date (required), value, source channel, last touch | Person/company; becomes a project when won |
| **Metric** (Scorecard) | Weekly target, owner, on/off track | Goal or area |
| Issue | Description, priority, status (IDS) | Creates tasks |
| Decision | Context, options, expected outcome, confidence %, review date | Area, project, issue |
| **Note** | Type (fleeting, source, idea, map, project, daily), links with a reason, status, summary layer | Resource, learning area, content piece |
| Resource | Format, why saved (required), status, progress | Learning area, notes |
| **Learning area** | Target performance, map of what/how to learn, skill nodes, weekly time budget, "things I don't know" list | Goal, content pillar, practice sessions, recall cards |
| **Content piece** | Pillar, platforms, format, hook text and type, status, parent/children, performance snapshots, lesson learned | Notes, swipes, learning area, deals (via source) |
| Swipe entry | URL, platform, hook (visual, spoken, on-screen text), format, structure, outlier ratio, why it worked (required) | Pillar, ideas, content pieces |
| Process (SOP) | Steps (7–15), owner, tools, last performed, ladder rung | Area, recurring tasks |

A few rules hold this model together. Each project belongs to exactly one Area and to at most one goal. Each task belongs to one project or one Area. "When" (the day you plan to work on it) stays separate from "deadline" (when it has a real consequence). Things 3 works this way: a deadline on a "Someday" item pulls it back into Today on that date ([MacPowerUsers](https://talk.macpowerusers.com/t/q-for-things-3-users-the-when-start-date/19654)). This stops fake due dates from turning into a wall of guilt. A single Person/Company record is shared by the pipeline, the personal CRM, client projects, commitments and meeting notes. Learning areas feed content pillars, and content pieces link back to their source notes and swipes and forward to deals through the source channel. Every object has an **owner and a visibility setting**. Areas, goals, Rocks, projects, metrics, processes, the pipeline and the content calendar can be shared later. The day page, journal, check-ins and habits stay private. This matters for the company layer: when personal goals align with the group's goal, group performance improves, and "without such alignment, personal goals have a detrimental effect on a group's performance" ([Locke & Latham 2002](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20%282002%29.pdf)). Company goals can later become parents of the founder's work-area goals with no change to the structure.

The **Day page** collects that day's time blocks, completed tasks, habit check-ins, energy check-in, journal lines and quick captures, with backlinks from everything touched that day. Logseq opens straight to today's journal page, which removes the "where do I start" decision ([It's FOSS](https://itsfoss.com/comparison/obsidian-vs-logseq/)).

### Notes and learning: write in your own words, then test yourself

The note systems that last share a few rules. The Zettelkasten keeps one idea per note, written in your own words, and asks for a reason on every link: "The explicit statement of the why is created knowledge." Its listed failures are notes that grow into "a bloated mess," links collected without intent, copy-pasting, and note-writing as a way to avoid real work ([zettelkasten.de](https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/)). Sönke Ahrens' "fleeting notes" should be processed or thrown away within a day or two ([ybrikman summary](https://www.ybrikman.com/blog/2023/08/10/how-to-take-smart-notes/)). Andy Matuschak advises "Prefer associative ontologies to hierarchical taxonomies," meaning links over folder trees, and "Write notes for yourself by default" ([Evergreen notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes)). Tiago Forte's Progressive Summarization adds layers (bold, then highlight, then a summary) only when you revisit a note anyway ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)). Nick Milo's "Mental Squeeze Point" is the moment a topic gets messy. That is when you build a Map of Content, a hub note that links the related notes ([LYT glossary](https://blog.linkingyourthinking.com/notes/lyt-glossary)). So the app should have six note types (fleeting, source, idea, map, project, daily) and a required one-line reason on each link. It should suggest a map when a cluster of notes has none. Folders and topic tags should not be required at setup.

Learning needs a different design from note-taking, because the evidence is clear about what works. Dunlosky and colleagues rated **practice testing and spaced practice as "high utility,"** self-explanation and interleaving as moderate, and **highlighting, summarizing and rereading as "low utility"** ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://iverson.cm.utexas.edu/courses/310M/Handouts/Dunlosky%20et%20al.%20-%202013%20-%20Improving%20Students%E2%80%99%20Learning%20With%20Effective%20Learni.pdf)). Most note apps reward exactly those low-utility habits. The best gap between reviews grows with how long you need to remember. For a test in 7 days the best gap was about 1 day, and for 70 days about 21 days, so roughly 10–20% of the retention period ([ERIC review](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED536925.pdf)). Anki's FSRS scheduler needs fewer reviews than the older SM-2 algorithm for the same retention ([Anki FAQ](https://docs.ankiweb.net/faqs/what-spaced-repetition-algorithm)). Scott Young's Ultralearning stresses mapping the subject first, learning by doing the real thing, and drilling your weakest point ([Scott H. Young](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/ultralearning/)). The Feynman technique has you explain a topic simply and go back to the source wherever your explanation stalls ([Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/feynman-learning-technique/)).

So a **Learning Area** should work like a project with a performance goal ("record a 60-second explainer with no notes"). It should hold a map of what to learn, skill nodes, a resource queue (each resource needs a "why saved" line, with one or two active at a time), practice sessions that log the gaps you found, and a daily recall queue of 10–15 minutes scheduled with FSRS and mixed across areas. A "review" mode should hide a note's body and ask you to recall it from its title before showing it. That turns browsing into practice testing. The app should give credit for recall, explanation and real practice, not for highlighting. Learning also feeds content. swyx's "learn in public" idea is to publish your "learning exhaust" while you learn ([swyx](https://swyx.io/learn-in-public)). The flow works like this. After a session, the app asks "What can you now explain?" and saves the answer as an idea note. A "make it public" action drafts a simple explanation, flags jargon as gaps, and creates short post variants as content seeds. Seeds that beat your usual performance get promoted to the weekly hub piece. Audience questions come back as new gaps to learn. Notes stay private by default, and publishing is a deliberate step.

### The content library treats saved videos as raw material, not a collection

A saved reel or post is worth something only if you break it down when you save it. Otherwise the swipe file becomes another case of the "Collector's Fallacy," the feeling that saving is the same as learning ([zettelkasten.de](https://www.zettelkasten.de/posts/collectors-fallacy)). Each swipe entry should record the hook text and its three layers (what you see, what is said, the on-screen text), the hook category (curiosity, contrarian, result, question, mistake, myth, list, story) ([quso.ai](https://quso.ai/blog/video-hook-ideas); [Kudozz](https://www.kudozz.in/blog/short-form-video-hooks)), the format and structure, and an **outlier ratio** (views ÷ the creator's usual views). It also needs two required fields, "why it worked" and "what to steal," plus a link to a content pillar. AI can transcribe the clip and pre-fill most fields, so the user only confirms and writes one line.

Your own pieces move through a status board: Idea → Seed (tested as a short post) → Brief → Script → Ready to record → Recorded → Editing → Ready to publish (a checklist gate) → Scheduled → Published → Repurposing → Measured → Recycle due (at +6 and +12 months) or Archived. Text posts skip the recording stages. Ali Abdaal reportedly keeps about 100 ideas in his bank so the pipeline never runs dry ([Judkin](https://judkin.com/ali-abdaal-creative-workflow-with-notion/)), and he spends "at least 10-20 minutes thinking about ONLY the first 10 seconds" before filming ([Ali Abdaal](https://aliabdaal.com/youtube/the-ultimate-guide-to-youtube/)). When a hub piece is published, the app should create linked child drafts (shorts, carousel, text posts, thread). Measure each post at 24 hours, 7 days and 30 days against **your own median**, not against raw numbers. YouTube itself compares a video to your last 10 similar videos and warns that a high click rate with low watch time signals clickbait ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217?hl=en&co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dvideo)). It also reports that half of channels have click rates between 2% and 10% ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en)). A weekly 20-minute content review should list your top and bottom three pieces, ask "why?", save a lesson, and move winning hooks into a personal hook library.

## Five rituals keep the system true

The flows matter more than the modules, because a system that is not reviewed stops being trusted. All proven systems use the same pattern: look back, get current, score, then commit to a few priorities.

| Ritual | Length | Core steps | Main sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capture | Seconds | One inbox, every channel, no filing decision | GTD, Mem, Todoist Ramble |
| Daily start-up | ≤15 min | Close yesterday, pick Top 3, capacity check, place blocks, one if-then plan | Sunsama, Full Focus, Newport, Gollwitzer |
| Shutdown | ≤10 min | Empty inbox, review lists and 2-week calendar, decide every leftover, ready-to-resume note, one progress line, "shutdown complete" | Newport, Leroy, Amabile |
| Weekly review | 60–90 min (20-min minimum mode) | Celebrate, score, reflect, get clear, get current (incl. pipeline and commitments), scorecard and issues, plan Big 3, share | GTD, Full Focus, 12 Week Year, Kazanjy, EOS |
| Monthly | 30–45 min | Re-rate areas, goal status, habit changes, time by domain, migrate stale tasks | GTD Areas, Bullet Journal, Guillebeau |
| Quarterly | 2–3 h | Grade goals, life-area scan, burnout check, next ≤3 goals with tactics and WOOP, prune the system | 12 Week Year, EOS, Wildeford |
| Annual | Half-day or more | Past Year Review, what went well/badly, goals by area, theme, plan Free Days first | Ferriss, Guillebeau, Forte, Sullivan |

### Capture: one inbox, no filing decision at capture time

Capture works because unfinished goals cause intrusive thoughts, and making a specific plan for them removed that effect in experiments by Masicampo and Baumeister ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/tech-support/201310/why-your-to-do-list-drives-you-crazy)). So every input should reach one inbox through the cheapest channel available: a global quick-add, the share sheet, email forwarding, a voice brain-dump, a phone widget, and offline capture. Mem 2.0's web clipper saves pages "with no filing decisions," because, as Mem puts it, many tools "make you work really hard just to stay organized" ([Mem](https://get.mem.ai/blog/mem-2-0)). Processing then follows GTD's clarify step: decide if it is actionable, define the next action, and do it now if it takes under two minutes ([GTD two-minute rule](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2020/05/the-two-minute-rule-2/)). Linear's triage shows a fast keyboard pattern for this: accept, mark duplicate, decline or snooze. Snoozed items come back as unread at the chosen time ([Linear Triage](https://linear.app/docs/triage)). Inbox items older than about 48 hours should prompt "process, turn into a task, or discard."

### Daily start-up: a capacity check beats a long list

Sunsama's guided plan is the reference. It reviews yesterday if you skipped shutdown, pulls tasks from your sources, totals the planned time against a workload limit you set, and lets you defer items or put them on the calendar. It advises new users to plan about **5.5 hours of work for a 9-to-5 day** with lunch ([Sunsama](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/daily-planning)). The founder version pulls from goal tactics, due commitments, sales next steps, habits, the calendar and learning blocks. Pick a Top 3 tagged by domain, normally with at least one delivery item and one revenue item. Let the user choose a daily shape: Big 3, Ivy Lee's six ranked items worked in order with leftovers carried over ([James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/ivy-lee)), or 1-3-5. Add one if-then plan for the biggest expected distraction. The ritual should take under three minutes of clicking.

Energy should inform the plan, but lightly. A one-tap morning check-in (sleep hours, sleep quality, energy 1–5, mood 1–5) is enough. Sleep matters for founders. In a study of 700+ entrepreneurs, less sleep led to less accurate judgments of a new venture idea's potential ([UCF](https://www.ucf.edu/news/study-sleep-is-essential-for-business-leaders-seeking-next-successful-venture/)). When sleep is under about six hours or energy is 2 or below, the app should offer a "low-energy day": Top 1 instead of Top 3, deep work moved, and habits at their minimum versions. Don't assume a chronotype. A review of 65 studies found that **over 80% showed no main effect of chronotype on thinking performance**, and the "best time of day" effect appeared in only 45% of studies of adults aged 18–45 ([Kingston University](https://researchinnovation.kingston.ac.uk/en/publications/chronotype-and-synchrony-effects-in-human-cognitive-performance-a/)). After two or three weeks of check-ins, the app can learn the person's own energy curve instead. For focus blocks, short breaks raised vigor (d = .36) and lowered fatigue (d = .35) but did not clearly improve performance ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f)). Planned breaks caused less fatigue than self-chosen breaks, with the same task completion ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)). So offer 50–90 minute blocks with a real break, keep Pomodoro as an option, and don't sell "90-minute ultradian cycles" as science, because that idea is contested ([Wikipedia: BRAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_rest%E2%80%93activity_cycle)). Days break often, so a one-tap re-plan matters. Newport re-draws his blocks when the day goes off-plan ([Cal Newport](https://calnewport.com/deep-habits-the-importance-of-planning-every-minute-of-your-work-day/)), and fixed plans can make people closed-minded and keep them on a plan that no longer makes sense ([University of Mannheim](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/forschung-erleben/artikel/lieber-planlos-als-verplant/)).

### Shutdown: give every loose end a plan, then stop

Cal Newport's shutdown ritual is the model. Move new items from collection points into your task lists, review all the lists, check the calendar for the **next two weeks**, update the weekly plan, then say "schedule shutdown, complete" ([Cal Newport 2009](https://www.calnewport.com/blog/2009/06/08/drastically-reduce-stress-with-a-work-shutdown-ritual/)). Three research-backed additions belong here. First, a **ready-to-resume note** on unfinished deep work (where you stopped, the next step). Task-switching leaves "attention residue," and such a plan reduces it ([Sophie Leroy](https://www.uwb.edu/business/faculty/sophie-leroy/attention-residue)). Second, a **one-line progress entry**. In about 12,000 diary entries, making progress was "by far the most prominent" feature of the best workdays, and setbacks hit two to three times harder than progress ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6685.html)). Third, a **lesson prompt**. Trainees who spent the last 15 minutes of each day reflecting on what they learned did 23% better on the final test ([HEC Paris](https://www.hec.edu/en/knowledge/instants/power-reflection-work-insight-hec-professor-giada-di-stefano)). Every leftover task then gets one decision: tomorrow, backlog or drop. Sunsama's guide also suggests rescheduling, delegating or deleting ([Sunsama](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/daily-shutdown)). Nothing should roll forward silently. Finally, "shutdown complete" should mute work notifications, because mental detachment from work is mainly linked to lower exhaustion ([recovery meta-analysis](https://scholarworks.sjsu.edu/faculty_rsca/3599)).

### Weekly review: one merged checklist, mostly pre-filled

The weekly review is the most important ritual, and it should merge four sources into one resumable checklist. Start by **celebrating wins** (pre-filled from completed tasks and progress lines), as Full Focus does ([Full Focus Weekly Preview](https://fullfocusplanner.com/wp-content/themes/fullfocusplanner-v6/files/quickstart/Weekly%20Preview%20Pages.pdf)). **Score** the week: Big 3 completion with partial credit, the 12 Week Year execution score, and habit consistency. **Reflect** with keep, improve, start and stop. **Get clear** by emptying the inboxes, then **get current** in GTD fashion ([GTD Weekly Review checklist](https://gettingthingsdone.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/Weekly_Review_Checklist.pdf)). Check the calendar two weeks back and four weeks ahead (Forte's range) ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/the-weekly-review-is-an-operating-system/)). Review the Waiting For list, confirm every active project has a next action and every deal has a dated next step, and handle "I owe" commitments first. Then fill in the **Scorecard** numbers as on or off track, work the top three **issues** with IDS, and look at Someday/Maybe. **Plan** next week's Big 3 and tactics, place maker blocks first, protect the Free Day, and run a WOOP on the riskiest item. Optionally, **share** the score with a co-founder, since public reporting strengthened the effect of progress tracking ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)).

Two design choices keep this review alive. Weekly planning should set **objectives, not a full schedule**. Sunsama refuses to pre-fill every day, "since it often leads to feeling crushed if you fall behind early in the week" ([Sunsama](https://roadmap.sunsama.com/changelog/weekly-planning-ritual)). And the review must be resumable and short in its minimum form. Forte does his in short sessions over two or three days and cut his checklist to five items: email, calendar, desktop, notes and tasks ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/the-weekly-review-is-an-operating-system/)). No evidence says which day is best. Dorsey used Sunday, Kazanjy's pipeline review is on Wednesday, and EOS only says "same day, same time." Friday afternoon is a sensible default for a founder.

### Monthly, quarterly and annual reviews tune direction, not tasks

The monthly review should fall on the 1st, because temporal landmarks trigger fresh starts: gym visits and goal commitments spike at the start of a week, a month and after a birthday ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)). Use it to re-rate each Life Area 1–10 (the Wheel of Life) ([MindTools](https://www.mindtools.com/ak6jd6w/the-wheel-of-life/)), mark goals on track or at risk, decide which habits to graduate, shrink or drop, compare hours by domain with your targets, and migrate stale tasks. The quarterly review closes a 12-week cycle. Grade the key results, look at average execution, list key successes and mistakes ([Peter Wildeford](https://peterwildeford.substack.com/p/my-template-for-a-quarterly-review)), run a short burnout check, set the next three goals or fewer with tactics and a WOOP, and prune: archive areas, habits and projects that no longer serve you. The annual review combines Tim Ferriss's Past Year Review with Chris Guillebeau's method. Ferriss goes through last year's calendar week by week, finds the top 20% of positive and negative peaks, books the positives now, and puts the negatives on a "Not-To-Do list" ([Tim Ferriss](https://tim.blog/2018/12/28/past-year-review/)). Guillebeau lists 6–8 things that went well and badly, treats **about 80% of goals achieved as healthy** (100% may mean the goals were too easy), sets 3–5 measurable goals per category, and picks a yearly theme last ([Chris Guillebeau](https://chrisguillebeau.com/how-to-conduct-your-own-annual-review/)). Following Sullivan, Free Days and holidays go into the new year's calendar first.

### Habits and routines: built to survive bad weeks

Habits take months, not 21 days. In Lally's study the median time to automaticity was **66 days, ranging from 18 to 254**. "Missing one opportunity… did not materially affect the habit formation process," but very inconsistent people did not form habits ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [Spring.org.uk](https://www.spring.org.uk/2023/01/form-a-habit.php)). A 2024 review found individual ranges of **4 to 335 days** ([University of Adelaide](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006)). Machine-learning analysis of more than 12 million gym check-ins found gym habits take months, and that time since the last visit predicts whether people come back ([Buyalskaya et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10151500)). Streaks motivate, but a broken streak makes people less likely to continue, even when the break was not their fault. The harm grows when people blame themselves and shrinks when the streak can be "repaired" ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)). Building planned skips ("emergency reserves") into a goal helped people reach their goals **up to 40% more often**, and 55% rebounded after a missed day vs 37% under a rigid daily goal ([UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/)). Duolingo's "Weekend Amulet," which protects a streak over the weekend, made users 4% more likely to return a week later ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals)).

James Clear's methods line up with this evidence. Anchor a new habit to a specific, frequent cue ("When I close my laptop for lunch," not "when I take a break") ([James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/habit-stacking)). Track one habit consistently rather than ten sporadically, scale habits down to two minutes, and "never miss twice" ([James Clear](https://jamesclear.com/habit-tracker)). BJ Fogg's model says behavior needs motivation, ability and a prompt at the same moment ([Fogg Behavior Model](https://behaviormodel.org/)). Note that "never miss twice" is a rule of thumb, not a tested limit. Lally only shows that a *single* miss did not matter. So every habit should require an if-then plan with a specific anchor, plus minimum, standard and stretch versions. Set the default target as "seven a week with two reserve skips," log each day as yes, no or skip, and allow a repair within 24–48 hours. Show "X of the last 30 days" ahead of the streak count, cap new active habits at one to three with a queue for the rest, and run an automaticity check at day 30, 60 and 90 to "graduate" habits to lighter tracking. A **routine** is different: it is a short checklist triggered by one anchor (start-up ≤15 minutes, shutdown ≤10), tracked as one unit, with a minimum version for bad days. Habits and focus blocks can float in the calendar like Reclaim's flexible habits ([Reclaim](https://partner.reclaim.ai/key-features-and-descriptions)). Tasks should stay where the user puts them.

## The Today screen and AI should remove work, not add it

### Open on Today, navigate by Area, capture from anywhere

The default screen should be **Today**: a task and commitment list on the left (with an evening or personal section, as in Things 3) and a time-blocked calendar on the right, with drag between the two. Structured puts tasks and events on one vertical timeline so gaps and overloads are visible at a glance ([Dave Swift](https://daveswift.com/structured/)). The sidebar should list the Life Areas plus fixed destinations: Inbox, Today, Week, Goals, Pipeline, Content, Library, Learning, People and Reviews. Each Area page shows its standard, rating, goals, active projects, habits and notes. Each Goal page shows the key-result chart next to the weekly execution trend. A founder dashboard shows the Scorecard, target vs actual hours by domain, pipeline coverage, content cadence and the promise-keeping rate. Use **views over the same objects**, not separate pages. Sales and content share one board component, goals and projects get a timeline, the library gets a table, and time blocks get a calendar. A command palette should create any object, jump anywhere and start any ritual, and it should show the keyboard shortcut each time so muscle memory builds, as in Linear ([925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/linear-design-breakdown-saas-ui-2026)). Check-offs and captures should feel instant through optimistic, local-first writes ([DEV](https://dev.to/0xgosu/why-linear-feels-fast-local-data-small-updates-and-product-discipline-1m53)).

Mobile needs its own design. The most common complaint about Notion is that its mobile app "essentially shrink[s] the desktop app onto a phone," that capture takes too many steps, and that users face "years of staring at loading icons" ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/finally-understand-why-people-leave-notion)). Notion only shipped offline mode in August 2025 ([Notion 2.53](https://www.notion.com/releases/2025-08-19)). On a phone, Today should collapse to Now, Next and Later, with one big capture button, habit check-ins and the shutdown. Planning and reviews can stay mainly on desktop. Keep the visuals calm, with no cover images or decoration that invites tinkering.

### AI: capture, filing and reviews first; autonomy last

The AI features with the most value are the ones that remove maintenance work. In order:

**Voice and natural-language brain-dumps that become structured items in a review tray.** Todoist's Ramble turns speech into tasks with dates, priorities and projects, and users review them before adding. Its team learned that easy correction matters more than first-time accuracy. Injecting the user's full project and label list into the prompt beat building a retrieval system, and the model had to be stopped from "over-interpreting" speech ([Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/building-todoist-ramble-how-doist-turned-voice-braindumps-into-real-time-task-capture/)). The same approach fits a life OS: pass the real list of areas, goals, projects and people, and let the model suggest where each item belongs.

**Auto-filing chips** suggest the area, goal, project or person for each capture as one-tap accept buttons.

**AI-drafted reviews** pre-fill wins, scores, slipped commitments, idle deals and habit consistency from data the app already has. The user edits instead of writing from scratch.

**Conversation routing.** Bot-free meeting note-takers are now common ([Tana](https://tana.inc/docs/tana-meeting-notetaker)), so a transcript alone is no advantage. The value is in routing: promises become "I owe" or "waiting for" commitments, people go into the pipeline, objections go into the content idea bank, and insights become notes.

**Meaning-based search and "heads up" resurfacing**, as in Mem 2.0 ([Mem](https://get.mem.ai/blog/mem-2-0)). Before a sales call or a learning block, the app shows the relevant past notes and history.

**Plan suggestions, not silent re-planning.** Motion's auto-scheduler is criticized for "a plan that keeps reshuffling itself" ([usecarly](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/motion-ai-review/)). Its own help center has a page for tasks "not scheduling where you expect" ([Motion help](https://help.usemotion.com/tasks-events-and-auto-scheduling-101/tasks-not-scheduling-where-you-expect-heres-whats-actually-happening)). Let only habits and focus blocks float. Offer moves like "you are 2 hours over capacity; defer these three?" and never move a locked item.

**Coaching flows** for goals and habits: a WOOP conversation, a goal-conflict check and a learning-goal suggestion. WOOP delivered through interaction beat WOOP delivered through documents (g = 0.465 vs 0.277) ([Wang et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8149892/)). An AI coach is probably closer to the interactive version, but no one has tested this in apps.

**Content and knowledge helpers**: transcribing swipes and pre-filling hook fields, drafting short posts from the hub piece, a Feynman-style simple draft that flags jargon, recall questions for the user to approve, link and map suggestions, and process drafts from a screen recording (Martell's Camcorder Method done by software). But the user should still write idea notes in their own words, because that is where the understanding comes from.

**Agents and an MCP server**, last. Notion 3.0's agents run multi-step tasks and use an instructions page as memory ([Notion 3.0](https://www.notion.com/releases/2025-09-18)). Akiflow launched an MCP connection in June 2026 so users can manage tasks from Claude, ChatGPT or Cursor ([Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/products/akiflow/launches/aki-2)). An MCP server lets the founder bring their own assistant.

Trust must be earned. Only 7% of desk workers consider AI output completely trustworthy for work tasks ([Slack Workforce Index](https://slack.com/intl/en-au/blog/news/the-workforce-index-june-2024)). So AI should be opt-in, show a preview before any bulk change, keep version history so you can undo, and use an editable instructions page. Personal patterns should be labeled "observations," not diagnoses. Private journal and brain-dump entries should not be summarized by AI unless the user turns that on.

## Abandonment is the failure mode to design against

The best-documented failure is quitting after reviews lapse. David Allen says partial use leaves commitments in your head. You stop trusting the system, you get driven by the "latest and loudest," and falling off is "almost universal" ([David Allen](https://davidallen.substack.com/p/partial-or-complete-gtd)). He also describes skipping the review for more than three weeks as the start of falling off ([Goodreads: David Allen blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/15358466-getting-back-on-the-wagon-with-gtd)). No source gives real abandonment rates for any system or app. That is a true gap. But the causes repeat across forums, press and studies, and each one has a design answer.

| Failure mode | What the evidence shows | Design defense |
|---|---|---|
| Review lapse | Falling off is "almost universal"; skipping 3+ weeks leads to abandonment | Short, pre-filled review with a 20-min minimum mode; catch-up mode after a gap; flags for projects with no next action, tasks untouched 30+ days, deals with no next step |
| Maintenance tax and tinkering | One Notion user spent mornings fixing filters and views ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/finally-understand-why-people-leave-notion)); "productivity pr0n" and the system as "Rube Goldberg device" ([Obsidian forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/how-do-you-manage-pornographic-productivity-in-obsidian/15906/20)); "Your system has to give you time, not take time" ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/para/)) | Fixed spine, no blank canvas, few custom fields, no decoration; show time spent planning vs doing |
| Templates built for an organized life | Life OS templates are "built for someone who already has their life organised" ([Fabric](https://developers.fabric.so/blog/why-people-are-leaving-notion)) | Start with 3–5 areas and Today only; unlock modules with use; onboard by archiving old material into a dated folder instead of sorting it ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/basboverview/)) |
| Over-commitment | Workers "keep saying yes" and confuse activity with useful work; the fix is "do fewer things" ([Cal Newport](https://calnewport.com/my-new-book-slow-productivity/)) | Daily capacity check; caps: ≤3 goals, ≤3 weekly and daily Big 3, ≤3–5 active projects, 1–3 new habits; overflow goes to Someday |
| Too many or conflicting goals | Goal conflict is linked to lower performance and well-being; goals narrow attention to what is measured ([Locke & Latham](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20%282002%29.pdf)) | Goal cap; conflict check when setting goals; monthly Area ratings as a balance check |
| Brittle plans | Fixed plans can make people closed-minded; pre-filled weeks leave people "crushed" | Weekly objectives, not daily schedules; one-tap re-plan; 20–30% buffer |
| Guilt and broken streaks | Broken streaks reduce persistence; Habitica users felt "punished… in especially productive times" ([LMU study](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/77668)); regret was the strongest predictor of intending to quit a gamified fitness app ([ITP study](https://unpaywall.org/10.1108%2FITP-08-2017-0271)) | Consistency % first, reserve skips, streak repair, yes/no/skip; no red overdue badges; bulk "reschedule all" ([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/plan-your-day-with-the-todoist-today-view-UVUXaiSs)); a "fresh start" after any absence instead of a wall of overdue items |
| Collector's fallacy | "you collected something doesn't mean you learned it" ([Stefan Imhoff](https://stefanimhoff.de/zettelkasten-note-taking-devonthink/)) | Required "why saved"; 48-hour inbox rule; captured-vs-processed ratio; swipes-used stat; 1–2 active resources per area |
| Slow, mobile-poor capture | Notion users fell back to other apps for capture; offline only arrived in 2025 | Local-first, offline capture, widget, voice |
| Lock-in fear | Akiflow reviewers flag no data export and lost tasks ([usecarly](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/akiflow-review/)) | Full export to Markdown, JSON and CSV from day one |
| Personal system meets company limits | Newport argues overload is an organizational problem that personal systems cannot fix ([Meredith Farkas](https://meredithfarkas.substack.com/p/the-productivity-trap)) | Shared commitments, owners and a weekly sync in the company layer |

Burnout deserves its own defenses, because founders are a higher-need group. In one survey of 242 entrepreneurs, **72% reported mental-health concerns** affecting them directly or through family history. Rates of depression (30%) and ADHD (29%) were higher than in the comparison group, though this was a convenience sample ([Freeman et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs11187-018-0059-8)). The WHO defines burnout by three dimensions: exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about work, and reduced professional efficacy ([KUOW/NPR](https://www.kuow.org/stories/who-redefines-burnout-as-a-syndrome-linked-to-chronic-stress-at-work)). Working **55 hours or more a week** is linked to higher risk of heart disease and stroke. WHO/ILO estimated 745,194 deaths from it in 2016 ([WHO/ILO via CDC](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/230297)). The app already holds the signals it needs. It can flag 55+ hours in two of the last three weeks, average sleep under 6.5 hours, no full day off in 14 days, shutdown completed on two or fewer of five workdays, a 7-day mood or energy average one point below the 4-week baseline, no recovery activity logged in two weeks, or a falling execution score alongside high hours. These thresholds are design proposals, not clinical cutoffs. A three-question monthly or quarterly pulse that maps to the WHO dimensions adds a direct check, though it is not a validated instrument. The response should be **protective, not more productivity pressure**. Suggest a lighter week with Top 1 instead of Top 3, propose a day off, enforce the evening shutdown, switch habits to their minimum versions automatically, write kind copy ("A missed day is normal; restart today"), and prompt "what can I delegate, automate or drop?" If severe or lasting low-mood signals appear, the app should show professional help and crisis resources and say clearly that it is not a clinical tool.

## Build in five phases, each one usable on its own

The build order should follow the failure analysis. The daily loop and the weekly review must work before anything else, because every other module depends on a system the founder trusts. AI arrives with the module it serves, not as a separate phase. The founder is the first user, so each phase should get at least a month of real daily use before the next one starts.

| Phase | Goal | Main build | AI added | Ready to move on when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Daily loop** | Trust the system every day | Inbox with all capture channels; 3–5 starter Areas; Projects; Tasks with "when" and deadline; Today screen (list + timeline); guided start-up with capacity check; shutdown; weekly review v1; Day page with check-in and short journal; command palette; offline; full export | Natural-language quick add; voice brain-dump into a review tray | Start-up and shutdown done on 4 of 5 workdays, and the weekly review done 4 weeks in a row |
| **2. Direction and rhythm** | Connect days to goals | Purpose and vision; annual and 12-week goals with key results and tactics; execution score; Ideal Week with maker/manager blocks and Free/Focus/Buffer days; habits and routines with reserves and repair; monthly, quarterly and annual reviews; Area ratings; caps; fresh-start flow | WOOP and goal coach; AI-drafted weekly review | One full 12-week cycle finished, with weekly scores and a quarterly review |
| **3. Founder engines** | Put revenue and promises in one place | People and companies; deal pipeline with required next step and idle flags; commitments ledger; relationship cadence; Scorecard (5–10 numbers); hours-by-domain dashboard; content pipeline, calendar, publish checklist and performance snapshots; swipe file and hook library; decision journal | Calls and meetings routed into commitments, deals and ideas; swipe transcription and tagging; drafts of short posts and follow-ups | No open deal without a dated next step for 4 straight reviews; one hub piece shipped every week for a month |
| **4. Knowledge, learning and delegation** | Turn input into skill, content and processes | Note types with link reasons and maps; resource queue; Learning Areas with skill nodes, practice sessions and an FSRS recall queue; learn-in-public flow; time-and-energy audit, DRIP, Buyback Rate; process library with ladder readiness; personal energy insights; burnout signals | Meaning-based search and resurfacing; link and map suggestions; recall questions; Feynman drafts; process drafts from recordings | Recall queue used weekly; delivery and lead-to-close processes written down |
| **5. Company layer** | Share what should be shared | Multiple users, owners and seats; shared Areas, goals, Rocks, metrics, pipeline, content and processes, with the personal layer kept private; co-founder weekly sync with IDS; quarterly planning; permissions. Accountability Chart, People Analyzer and fuller meeting rhythms only at about 10+ people | Agents with an instructions page, preview-before-apply and version history; MCP server | A first hire or contractor runs one ladder rung from written processes |

Phases 2 and 3 can swap places. If revenue is the urgent problem, the sales pipeline needs only People, Tasks and the Today screen from Phase 1, and the founder gains more from dated next steps than from a vision page. Knowledge and learning come fourth on purpose, even though notes matter. Note systems are where tinkering and the collector's fallacy do the most damage, and a simple Day journal and quick notes already exist from Phase 1, with the swipe file arriving in Phase 3. The same order should apply *inside* the product for new users. They see Today and the Inbox first. The weekly review unlocks after a week of daily planning, goals after the first review, and the business modules when the user turns on "Business." The system should also shrink on purpose. Experienced users report that their systems contract as they drop workflows that "weren't practical," and they ask "What tools can I eliminate?" ([MacPowerUsers](https://talk.macpowerusers.com/t/notion-life-operating-systems/17809)). Pruning belongs in every quarterly review as a normal, good outcome.

## Conclusion

The product at the center of this design is the weekly review, not a module or a framework. Every module either feeds it (captures, check-ins, deals, commitments, content metrics) or is changed by it (goals, blocks, habits, the plan for next week). The real test of the app is simple: is the founder still doing the weekly review in month six, and does every open deal and promise still have a date? That changes how to build it. A feature that adds input without making the review shorter or more pre-filled is a cost, not a gain. And because no branded system has been tested as a whole, the app's own data becomes the founder's evidence. Execution score vs outcomes, sleep vs energy, content vs leads, and planned vs actual hours by domain turn the life OS into a personal lab where the founder can test which rules actually work for them.

The path from personal tool to company system does not need a rewrite. It needs three early choices: an owner field on every goal, metric, commitment and process; a clear split between shareable and private objects; and a delegation module that turns repeated work into written processes. Together they make the founder's own habits the company's first operating system. The sales and content engines share one board component and one People record, so they can be designed once and reused. The delegation module is where the solo founder's time audit becomes the company's first job description.
